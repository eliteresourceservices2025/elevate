## Module 1: HIPAA foundations and your role at ERS

_Why HIPAA exists, the three rules and how they fit together, who it covers, and where ERS and you sit in the chain that protects patient information._

About 15 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials, refresher.

Key points:
- HIPAA exists so people can trust that their health information is used for their care and not for anything else.
- Three rules matter at work: the Privacy Rule, the Security Rule and the Breach Notification Rule.
- Covered entities hold patient information; business associates and their subcontractors handle it for them; you are part of ERS's workforce.
- A chain of agreements carries the same protections from the clinic to the last person who touches the information.
- Being able to open information is not the same as being allowed to use it.
- When you are not sure, stop, do not share, and ask your lead.
- ERS runs a privacy and security program, and training is one required part of it.

### 1.1 Why this training matters

Many ERS clients are doctors' offices, clinics, billing companies and other healthcare organizations. When you work for them, you may see information about real patients: who they are, what is wrong with them, and what they owe.

That information belongs to the patient. A United States law called **HIPAA** sets how it must be protected, and it applies to you from your first day of work.

> **INFO: How this course works**  
> Every lesson explains the rule, why it exists, and what it looks like in a normal workday. Short worked cases let you think before you see the answer. Your progress is saved, so you can stop and come back.

### 1.2 The problem HIPAA solved

Before HIPAA, there was no national standard for who could see a person's health records or what they could do with them. Records moved on paper and, increasingly, by computer, and the protection a patient had depended on where they lived and who held the file.

Two things made that a growing problem. First, health information is among the most sensitive information a person has: it can affect their job, their insurance, their relationships and how they are treated. Second, the healthcare system needs that information to move quickly between doctors, labs and insurers, or care suffers.

> **TIP: The balance**  
> HIPAA does not try to stop information from moving. It tries to make sure it moves only for the right reasons, to the right people, with the right protection.

### 1.3 What HIPAA is, and who enforces it

HIPAA is the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, a United States federal law passed in 1996. Its rules about health information are written and enforced by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), through its **Office for Civil Rights (OCR)**.

ERS is in the Philippines, but the work you do for U.S. clients falls under these rules through the contracts ERS signs. A client's patients are protected the same way whether the work is done across the street or across the world.

> **INFO**  
> HIPAA is also the floor, not the ceiling. Some state laws and client rules ask for more, and when they do, the stricter rule is the one you follow.

### 1.4 The three rules and how they fit

Three HIPAA rules matter most in daily work. Think of them as three questions:

**Three rules, three questions**
- **Privacy Rule**: Who may use or share health information, for what reasons, and what rights does the patient have over it?
- **Security Rule**: How must information held or sent electronically be kept safe from the people who should not have it?
- **Breach Notification Rule**: When information is exposed anyway, who must be told, and by when?

They work together. The Privacy Rule decides what is allowed. The Security Rule protects electronic information so only the allowed things happen. The Breach Notification Rule says what to do when something goes wrong.

### 1.5 Who is who: covered entities and business associates

HIPAA does not apply to everyone who ever sees health information. It applies to specific kinds of organizations, and to the people who work for them.

**The labels you will hear**

| Label | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covered entity | A health plan, a healthcare clearinghouse, or a healthcare provider that sends certain transactions electronically | A clinic that bills insurance |
| Business associate | A company that does work for a covered entity and handles patient information while doing it | A billing company, or ERS |
| Subcontractor | A company a business associate uses to do part of that work | A cloud service the billing company uses |
| Workforce member | A person whose work is under the organization's direct control, paid or not | An ERS team member, including contractors |

> **TIP**  
> Depending on the client, ERS is a business associate or a subcontractor. Either way, the same protections follow the information to you.

### 1.6 The chain of trust

A clinic cannot simply hand patient information to a vendor and hope for the best. The law builds a chain: every organization that handles the information signs an agreement promising to protect it, and must make the next organization in the chain promise the same.

1. **The clinic or plan**: The covered entity that first holds the information.
2. **ERS**: A business associate, bound by its agreement with the client.
3. **Anyone ERS uses**: Subcontractors, tools and vendors, bound by the same promise.
4. **You**: A member of ERS's workforce, bound by ERS's rules.

> **INFO**  
> The chain only holds if every link does. A rule your client gives you is not the client's own preference: it is the promise that came down the chain, and you are the last link.

### 1.7 Worked case: the helpful tool

**Case: A free transcription tool**

You are asked to type up a recorded voicemail from a clinic's patient line. The clinic's approved dictation tool is slow, so you find a free online transcription site that does it in seconds. You are about to upload the audio.

_Question:_ Why is this a problem, even though the site seems harmless?

_Answer:_ The site is not in the chain. ERS has no agreement with it, so nothing binds it to protect the recording, to limit what it does with it, or to tell anyone if it leaks. Uploading the audio is a disclosure to an outside company the client never approved. Use only the tool your client approved, and ask your lead if it is too slow.

### 1.8 Access is not permission

Your login may open a whole patient list. That does not mean you may look at every record. You may use information only for the task you were given.

**Allowed**
- Opening a record to complete the task your client assigned
- Using the approved system the client set up
- Sharing only with people your client named for that task

**Not allowed**
- Looking up a neighbor, friend or celebrity
- Copying records to your own phone or email
- Telling anyone about a patient, even "just a little"

> **WARNING**  
> Looking at records out of curiosity is a privacy violation, even if you never tell anyone and even if the system let you in. Access is logged, and logs are reviewed.

### 1.9 The agreement sets the rules

ERS and each client sign a **business associate agreement (BAA)**. It says what ERS may do with patient information, what safeguards are required, and how problems must be reported. You will study its contents in Module 5.

You do not need to read the contract. You do need to follow the instructions your lead and your client give you, because they come from it.

**Check yourself:** Where do you find out what you may do with a client's patient information?

- (correct) In your client's instructions and ERS's rules, as explained by your lead  
  _Right. Those are the rules that apply to your daily work._
- Whatever the system lets you open  
  _Being able to open something does not make it allowed._
- Whatever a coworker says is usually fine  
  _Habits spread, but they are not rules. Ask your lead._

### 1.10 How ERS keeps this program running

Following HIPAA is not only about individuals. Organizations must run a privacy and security program, and ERS does. You will see its parts in your daily work:

- **A named privacy contact**: the Privacy Officer, who owns the rules and answers questions.
- **Written policies and agreements**: ERS's policies, and each client's business associate agreement.
- **Risk checks**: regular reviews of where information could be exposed, and fixes.
- **Training**: HIPAA requires every workforce member to be trained, and ERS repeats it on a schedule.
- **Sanctions and reporting**: consequences for breaking the rules, and a way to report problems.
- **Records**: policy and training records are kept for six years.

> **TIP**  
> You are not expected to run the program. Your part is to follow it, ask when unsure, and report problems.

### 1.11 How HIPAA grew up

HIPAA did not arrive all at once. The steps explain why ERS, a business associate, is held to the rules directly:

**From 1996 to today**
1. **1996**: HIPAA becomes federal law.
2. **2003**: Organizations must comply with the Privacy Rule, which governs how information is used and shared.
3. **2009**: The HITECH Act makes business associates directly responsible for the Security Rule and strengthens enforcement.
4. **2013**: The Omnibus Rule folds HITECH into the HIPAA rules and adds detail on business associates and breach notification.

> **INFO**  
> Since 2009, ERS answers to the government for its own compliance, not only to its clients.

### 1.12 Why it matters to you

Mistakes happen, and ERS wants to hear about them quickly (more on that in Module 7). Deliberately misusing information is different.

- Civil penalties are enforced by HHS's Office for Civil Rights and can fall on organizations, and in some cases on individuals.
- Knowingly misusing patient information is a crime the Department of Justice prosecutes: up to 1 year in prison, 5 with false pretenses, 10 if for gain or harm.
- ERS has sanctions for breaking its privacy and security rules, up to ending your engagement.
- Clients can end their contract with ERS, which affects every teammate.

> **INFO**  
> ERS does not punish good-faith reports, and HIPAA prohibits retaliating against people who file a complaint about a possible violation. Speak up.

### 1.13 Try it: the familiar name

**Scenario: A name you know**

While doing your assigned task you see a patient name that matches your cousin's. Your login shows the full chart, and nobody would ever know.

- Open the chart to check whether it is really your cousin.  
  _That would be curiosity, not your task. Looking is a violation even if nobody finds out._
- (best) Finish only the task you were assigned and do not open anything extra.  
  _Correct. Use only what the task needs._
- Mention it to a friend so they can check on your cousin.  
  _Telling anyone about a patient is a disclosure. It is never okay._

### 1.14 The safest default

You will sometimes meet a situation nobody trained you for. There is one move that is always right:

1. **Stop**: Do not send, open, copy or say anything yet.
2. **Do not share**: Keep the information where it is.
3. **Ask**: Ask your team lead or your client contact for the approved way.

> **TIP**  
> Asking is never a mistake. A question costs a few minutes. A wrong disclosure cannot be taken back.

**Terms**

- **OCR**: The Office for Civil Rights at HHS, which enforces the HIPAA rules.
- **Covered entity**: A health plan, a healthcare clearinghouse, or a healthcare provider that sends certain transactions electronically.
- **Business associate**: A company that handles patient information while doing work for a covered entity.
- **Subcontractor**: A company that a business associate uses to do part of its work.
- **Workforce member**: A person under an organization's direct control, paid or not.
- **BAA**: Business associate agreement: the contract that sets what a business associate may do with patient information.

## Module 2: PHI, ePHI and identifiers

_What makes information protected health information, the 18 identifiers, how information can be de-identified, and what HIPAA does not treat as PHI._

About 12 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials, refresher.

Key points:
- PHI is health information that identifies a person, held or sent by a covered entity or business associate, in any form.
- ePHI is PHI in electronic form: databases, email, files, screenshots, voicemails and recordings.
- Names are only one of eighteen kinds of identifier, and context can identify a person without one.
- Taking off a name does not de-identify information; HIPAA has two specific methods.
- Some records are not PHI, such as an employer's employment records, but they can still need protection.
- Spoken and paper information is PHI too.

### 2.1 What is PHI?

**Protected health information (PHI)** is health information that identifies a person, or could reasonably be used to identify them, when it is held or sent by a covered entity or a business associate.

Think of it as three parts that come together: it is **about a person**, it is about their **health, their care or the payment for their care**, and it **identifies them**. Take away any one part and it may no longer be PHI.

> **TIP**  
> Health information means more than diagnoses. A sign-in sheet that shows who visited a clinic, an invoice, and a reminder call about an appointment all say something about a person's health care.

### 2.2 What counts as health information

HIPAA's definition covers information that relates to any of three things:

- The person's past, present or future **physical or mental health or condition**
- The **care** provided to the person
- The past, present or future **payment** for that care

That is why a billing record counts as much as a chart. If it says who a person is and connects them to care or payment for it, it is PHI.

### 2.3 ePHI: electronic PHI

**ePHI** is PHI in electronic form. The Security Rule applies to it.

- Records in an EHR, a billing system or a spreadsheet
- Email, chat messages and text messages
- Scanned documents, photos and screenshots
- Voicemail, call recordings and video calls
- Anything stored on a laptop, phone or drive

> **INFO**  
> The same fact can be PHI in three forms at once: on the screen, in a printout, and spoken aloud. The protection follows the information, not the format.

### 2.4 What identifies a person (1 of 2)

HIPAA lists **eighteen identifiers**. If health information carries any of them, it points to a person. The first nine:

**Identifiers 1 to 9**

| Identifier | Plain-language note |
| --- | --- |
| Names | Full or partial, including a nickname that points to one person |
| Small geographic areas | Street address, city, county, ZIP code: anything smaller than a state |
| Dates tied to the person | Birth, admission, discharge, death and any other date linked to them; ages over 89 |
| Telephone numbers | Mobile, home and office |
| Fax numbers | Often forgotten: faxes are common in healthcare |
| Email addresses | Personal or work |
| Social security numbers | Full or any part used to identify |
| Medical record numbers | Chart and patient ID numbers |
| Health plan beneficiary numbers | Member and policy IDs |

### 2.5 What identifies a person (2 of 2)

The other nine, which people forget most often. The list applies to the identifiers of the person and also to those of their relatives, employers and household members:

**Identifiers 10 to 18**

| Identifier | Plain-language note |
| --- | --- |
| Account numbers | Billing and payment accounts |
| Certificate or license numbers | Professional or driver's license numbers |
| Vehicle identifiers | License plates and vehicle serial numbers |
| Device identifiers and serial numbers | A medical device's unique number |
| Web addresses (URLs) | A link that leads to a person's own page or record |
| IP addresses | The address of the computer used |
| Biometric identifiers | Fingerprints and voice prints |
| Full-face photographs | And comparable images that show the face |
| Any other unique number, code or characteristic | A catch-all for anything that identifies one person |

### 2.6 Context changes the answer

A name is not the only way to point at a person. A rare diagnosis in a small town, a job title at a small company, or a unique combination of age, ZIP code and visit date can identify someone without any name at all.

**Case: The unnamed patient**

A team member posts in a public group: "A 91-year-old woman in our small town was admitted yesterday after her fall at the bakery. I handled her insurance claim." No name is given.

_Question:_ Is this PHI, and is the post a problem?

_Answer:_ Yes. It describes a person's care and payment, and in a small town the age, the event and the place make her easy to identify. Removing the name does not make it safe. Posting it is an impermissible disclosure, and it should be reported to your lead and HR.

### 2.7 Removing a name is not enough

Information is **de-identified** only when there is no reasonable way to tell who it is about. HIPAA recognizes exactly two methods, and neither is "I took the name out."

**Safe Harbor**
- Remove all 18 identifiers
- The organization has no actual knowledge that what is left could identify someone

**Expert Determination**
- A qualified statistician studies the data
- They document that the risk of identifying anyone is very small

> **WARNING**  
> De-identifying is the client's decision and process. A VA does not de-identify information on their own and send it onward as "safe."

### 2.8 What is not PHI

HIPAA's definition has deliberate exclusions. These are not PHI under HIPAA:

- Employment records that a covered entity holds in its role as an employer
- Education records covered by a different federal law (FERPA)
- Information that has been properly de-identified
- Records of a person who has been dead for more than 50 years

> **TIP: Careful**  
> Not being PHI does not mean not sensitive. An employer's sick-leave note is not PHI, but your client still expects it to be protected, and other laws may apply.

### 2.9 PHI is not only in the EHR

People expect PHI in the chart. It is just as often in the places around the chart, and that is where mistakes happen:

- A screenshot someone saves "to remember how to do it"
- A file name that includes a patient's name
- An email subject line or a calendar invite
- A sticky note, a notebook or a printed schedule
- A voicemail greeting or a call you take in a shared space

### 2.10 Try it: the screenshot

**Scenario: A question for the group chat**

You are stuck on a field in the client's billing system. You take a screenshot of your screen, which shows a patient's name, date of birth and balance, and want to ask a teammate in a group chat how to fill it in.

- Post the screenshot but crop out the name.  
  _The date of birth and balance still identify a person, and the chat may not be approved for PHI._
- (best) Describe the field in words without any patient details, or ask through the client's approved channel.  
  _Correct. You can get the answer without sending any PHI._
- Post it in a private chat with one teammate instead.  
  _A smaller audience is still a disclosure through an unapproved channel._

### 2.11 Spoken and paper PHI

The rules do not stop at the screen. Information you hear on a call, read on paper or see on a whiteboard is PHI, and you protect it the same way.

- Keep your voice down on calls, and take them where others cannot hear.
- Do not leave paper out. Put it away or destroy it the approved way.
- Do not describe a patient to friends or family, even without a name.
- Do not read details aloud in a shared or public space.

### 2.12 Quick recognition

**Check yourself:** Which of these is PHI when it is held by ERS for a clinic? (select all that apply)

- (correct) A list of appointment dates with patient initials and phone numbers  
  _Initials plus a phone number and appointment dates identify patients and say they receive care._
- (correct) A spreadsheet of invoice numbers and balances with patient account numbers  
  _An account number is an identifier and the balance relates to payment for care._
- A count of how many appointments the clinic had this month, with no names or dates  
  _A bare count does not identify anyone, so it is not PHI._

### 2.13 Terms from this module

**Terms**
- **PHI**: Protected health information: health information that identifies a person, held or sent by a covered entity or business associate.
- **ePHI**: PHI in electronic form, such as files, email, messages and recordings.
- **Identifier**: A piece of information that points to one person. HIPAA lists eighteen.
- **De-identified**: Information that cannot reasonably be used to identify a person, by one of HIPAA's two methods.
- **Safe Harbor**: The method of removing all 18 identifiers, with no knowledge that what is left could identify someone.
- **Expert Determination**: The method where a qualified expert documents that the risk of identification is very small.

## Module 3: The Privacy Rule: using and sharing patient information

_When patient information may be used or shared, when a written authorization is needed, how the public-interest exceptions work, and how to share only the minimum._

About 20 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials, refresher.

Key points:
- Use means working with information inside the organization; disclosure means giving it to someone outside; both need a valid reason.
- Treatment, payment and healthcare operations are permitted without asking the patient each time; most other purposes need a written authorization.
- Marketing with patient information and selling it generally need the patient's written authorization.
- The public-interest disclosures (police, courts, public health and others) are the client's decisions, never yours.
- Minimum necessary means the least information that does the job.
- Verify who is asking before you share anything.
- Use the ERS 5-point send check: who, what, why, where, how.

### 3.1 The privacy mindset

The Privacy Rule gives patients control over their information. Your job is not to decide what is safe to share. Your job is to use the information only for the task, and to share it only through the approved way.

> **INFO**  
> A good test before any action: "Would this patient be comfortable if they saw exactly what I am doing?"

### 3.2 Use, disclosure and request

These three words mean different things, and the rules are different for each.


| Word | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use | Working with information inside your organization to do your task | Reading a claim to correct it |
| Disclosure | Giving or showing information to someone outside the organization | Sending an invoice to a collection agency |
| Request | Asking another organization for patient information | Asking a lab for a result |

Each one needs a valid reason, and each one must be limited to what is needed.

### 3.3 The starting point: only what the rule allows

The Privacy Rule works like a locked door. Patient information may be used and disclosed only in the ways the rule lists. Everything else needs the patient's written permission.

**Three groups of reasons**
- **Always allowed**: To the patient, and to HHS when it investigates.
- **Allowed without a form**: Treatment, payment, healthcare operations, and a short list of public-interest and legal reasons.
- **Needs written authorization**: Almost everything else, including marketing and sale of the information.

### 3.4 Treatment, payment and operations

Covered entities may use and share information for **treatment**, **payment** and **healthcare operations** without asking the patient each time. Most of the work you do falls under these.


| Purpose | What it covers | Work you might do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Treatment | Providing, coordinating or managing a patient's care | Scheduling a referral appointment |
| Payment | Billing, claims, eligibility checks, collection | Verifying insurance coverage |
| Operations | Running the practice: quality review, training, audits | Preparing records for an internal audit |

> **WARNING**  
> That permission belongs to the covered entity and reaches ERS only through the BAA. It does not make everything fair game: you work within your client's specific instructions.

### 3.5 When the patient gets a chance to agree or object

A few disclosures are allowed if the patient is told and does not object. These are the covered entity's decisions, made with the patient, not by a VA on the phone.

- Telling visitors a patient's location in a facility, when the patient has not objected
- Sharing relevant information with a family member or friend involved in the patient's care or payment
- Notifying family in a disaster to help with care or location

> **TIP**  
> If the patient is present and says no, the answer is no. If you do not know whether they agreed, you do not share: you take a message and pass it to the client.

### 3.6 When the patient's written permission is needed

For anything outside treatment, payment, healthcare operations and the other purposes the rule permits, the patient must give a **written authorization**: a signed form that says what may be shared, with whom, and why.

**What a valid authorization contains**

| Element | Why it is there |
| --- | --- |
| A specific description of the information | So everyone knows exactly what may be shared |
| Who may share it, and who may receive it | So it goes only to the named people |
| The purpose | So it is not used for something else |
| An expiration date or event | So it does not last forever |
| Signature and date | So it is the patient's own choice |
| The right to revoke, in writing | So the patient can change their mind |

> **WARNING**  
> You will never decide this yourself. Your client keeps the authorizations and decides. If a request is outside your task, route it to your lead or client contact.

### 3.7 Marketing and selling patient information

**Marketing** means communicating about a product or service to encourage people to buy or use it. When a communication uses patient information for marketing, the patient generally must sign an authorization first. **Selling** patient information, receiving payment for it, also needs the patient's authorization.

**Usually fine**
- A reminder about the patient's own appointment
- A message about the clinic's own services as part of their care
- A face-to-face conversation about a product

**Needs authorization**
- Using a patient list to promote another company's product
- Taking money from a company to send its offer to patients
- Posting a patient's photo or story on a website or social media

> **WARNING**  
> If you build or maintain a client's website, newsletter or social pages, this applies to you directly. A patient's story, photo or review is not yours to post without the client confirming a signed authorization.

### 3.8 Psychotherapy notes

**Psychotherapy notes** are a counselor's own private notes about a counseling session, kept separate from the rest of the medical record. They get stronger protection: most uses and disclosures need a specific written authorization.

> **INFO**  
> This is different from the ordinary mental health information in a chart, such as medications, session times, test results and diagnoses. You will not usually see psychotherapy notes, and if you do, do not copy, quote or forward them. Ask your lead.

### 3.9 When police, a court or an agency asks

The law lets, and sometimes requires, a covered entity to share information without the patient's authorization in a defined list of situations:

- When another law requires it
- For public health activities, such as reporting certain diseases
- To report abuse, neglect or domestic violence, in the situations the rule describes
- For health oversight, such as an audit or license review
- In court and administrative proceedings, with an order, subpoena or similar process
- For certain law enforcement purposes, with limits
- To avert a serious and imminent threat to health or safety

Those decisions are the client's, made with its own legal checks. A phone call is not proof of any of them.

### 3.10 Worked case: the detective on the phone

**Scenario: A caller says he is a detective**

A man phones the clinic line, says he is a detective investigating a case, and asks for a patient's home address "urgently". He gives a badge number.

- Give the address. Police have the right to it.  
  _You cannot verify him, and whether the clinic may share it is the client's decision._
- (best) Share nothing, take his contact details, and pass the request to your client contact straight away.  
  _Correct. The client verifies and decides. You route._
- Confirm only whether the person is a patient.  
  _Confirming that someone is a patient is itself a disclosure._

**Case: A second look: the subpoena in the mail**

A letter arrives at the clinic from a law firm, with a document that looks like a subpoena asking for a patient's full chart. The office manager is out and the letter says it is due in three days.

_Question:_ Who handles this, and what do you do with it?

_Answer:_ The client's privacy contact or legal advisor handles it, because a subpoena alone may not be enough to release a chart without further steps. You do not copy, scan or send anything. Photograph nothing, note only that it arrived and when, and pass the original to your client contact the same day.

### 3.11 Minimum necessary

The **minimum necessary** rule means using, sharing or asking for only the least amount of information needed to do the job.

**Minimum necessary**
- Send the invoice number and amount
- Share only the dates needed to schedule
- Open the one record you were asked to update

**Too much**
- Send the full chart to answer a billing question
- Copy the entire patient list "to be safe"
- Browse other patients on the same screen

### 3.12 Where minimum necessary does not apply

The standard does not apply in a few places, because limiting the information would defeat the purpose:

- Disclosures to a provider for the patient's treatment
- Disclosures to the patient themselves
- Disclosures made under the patient's own authorization
- Disclosures to HHS for an investigation
- Disclosures the law requires

> **TIP**  
> These exceptions are for your client to apply, not for you to decide. If you think one applies, ask your lead or client contact. Do not rely on your own reading.

### 3.13 Incidental disclosures: when it cannot be perfect

The Privacy Rule does not demand that every possible exposure be eliminated. Some exposure comes with normal work: a name on a sign-in sheet, a conversation that someone nearby might hear, a screen that a visitor glances at. These are **incidental** and are allowed, but only if **reasonable safeguards** are in place and the information was limited to the minimum.

- Keep the reason for a visit off any sheet others can read.
- Lower your voice, move away from others, or use a headset.
- Angle or lock your screen so passers-by cannot read it.
- If you could say it without a name, say it without a name.

**Check yourself:** An incidental disclosure is acceptable when:

- (correct) Reasonable safeguards were in place and only the minimum information was involved  
  _Right. Safeguards and minimum necessary are what make it incidental._
- Nobody complained  
  _Complaints are not the test. Safeguards and minimum necessary are._
- The person overhearing was a coworker  
  _Who overhears is not the test either._

### 3.14 HIPAA is the minimum, not the maximum

HIPAA is a federal floor. Where a state law gives patients **more** privacy protection, that stricter law can apply as well. Some kinds of information, such as mental health, substance use treatment or HIV records, often have extra rules.

- Your client and ERS decide which rules apply to a task. Their instructions already reflect them.
- If a request involves sensitive records or something unusual, stop and ask.
- A rule being different in another state is a reason to ask, not a reason to guess.

> **TIP**  
> Whichever rule is stricter is the one you follow.

### 3.15 Know who you are talking to

Before you share anything, you must be sure the person is allowed to receive it. HIPAA requires organizations to verify the identity and authority of people requesting information when they are not already known. A friendly voice, an official-looking email or an urgent tone is not proof.

1. **Confirm who is asking**: Use your client's verification steps, not the details the caller gives you.
2. **Confirm they are allowed**: Is this person on the approved list for this information?
3. **Share the minimum**: Only what answers the question.

### 3.16 The ERS 5-point send check

Before you send any patient information, ask five questions. If any answer is "not sure", do not send.

1. **Who?**: Is the recipient the right, authorized person?
2. **What?**: Is it only the minimum information needed?
3. **Why?**: Is there a task-related reason?
4. **Where?**: Is the destination the approved system or address?
5. **How?**: Is the method approved and secure for this information?

### 3.17 Try it: the worried daughter

**Scenario: A call from a family member**

Someone calls the clinic line and says she is a patient's daughter. She is crying and asks for her mother's appointment details and what medication was prescribed.

- Give her the details, because she is clearly upset and family.  
  _You cannot confirm she is allowed to receive them. Sympathy is not authorization._
- (best) Kindly explain you cannot share information, and follow the client's process for such requests, such as taking a message for the clinic.  
  _Correct. Stay kind, share nothing, and use the approved process._
- Share only the appointment time, not the medication.  
  _Even "just the time" is PHI. Without confirmed permission you do not share it._

### 3.18 Terms from this module

**Terms**
- **Use**: Working with patient information inside an organization to do a task.
- **Disclosure**: Giving or showing patient information to someone outside the organization.
- **Authorization**: The patient's signed permission for a specific use or disclosure.
- **Minimum necessary**: Using, sharing or requesting only the least information needed for the purpose.
- **Incidental disclosure**: Exposure that comes with normal work despite reasonable safeguards, and is allowed.
- **Verification**: Confirming a person's identity and authority before sharing information.

## Module 4: Patients' rights over their information

_The rights HIPAA gives patients: notice, access, amendment, accounting of disclosures, restrictions, confidential communications and complaints, and what you do when one is raised._

About 15 minutes. Included in tracks: full.

Key points:
- Patients have rights to see, copy, correct and learn about disclosures of their own information.
- Every right has a deadline and a procedure, and the covered entity (your client) handles each request.
- The notice of privacy practices tells patients what the clinic does with their information and what their rights are.
- A personal representative stands in the patient's place, but who counts as one is the client's decision.
- Patients may complain to the clinic or to HHS, and cannot be punished for it.
- Your part: be kind, take down only the request, and pass it on the way your client instructs.

### 4.1 Why patients have rights

The information in a patient's record is about them. The Privacy Rule therefore gives patients rights over it: to see it, to correct it, to know who it was shared with, and to ask for it to be handled differently.

These rights are against the **covered entity**, your client. Often a patient does not know that. They call the number they have, and the person who answers may be you.

> **INFO**  
> You do not grant or refuse any right. You make sure the request reaches the people who do, quickly and without spreading the patient's information.

### 4.2 The rights at a glance


| Right | What the patient can ask for |
| --- | --- |
| Notice | A plain-language statement of how the clinic uses their information and what their rights are |
| Access | To see and get a copy of their own records |
| Amendment | To ask for a correction to information they believe is wrong |
| Accounting of disclosures | A list of certain times their information was shared |
| Restriction | To ask the clinic to limit how it uses or shares information |
| Confidential communications | To be contacted by a different method or at a different place |
| Complaint | To raise a concern with the clinic or with HHS |

### 4.3 The notice of privacy practices

Most providers must give patients a **notice of privacy practices** (NPP) at their first service, post it where patients can see it, and put it on their website if they have one. It explains how the clinic may use and share information, what rights the patient has, and who to contact.

- It is the clinic's document, not ERS's. You do not rewrite or reinterpret it.
- If a patient asks for a copy, give them the client's version through the client's approved way.
- If the website is one of your tasks, the notice must stay available there.

> **TIP**  
> When a patient asks "what do you do with my information?", the honest answer is "the clinic's notice explains it". Point them to it.

### 4.4 The right of access

Patients have the right to see and get a copy of the information in their **designated record set**: medical and billing records, and other records the clinic uses to make decisions about them. The clinic generally must respond **within 30 days**, with one 30-day extension if it explains why in writing.

**What the patient can expect**
- **A copy**: In the form they ask for, if the clinic can readily produce it that way, including electronic form.
- **A reasonable fee**: Limited to the cost of copying, supplies and postage, and any summary the patient agreed to.
- **Sent to someone else**: The patient can direct the copy to another person they name in writing.
- **A written reason if refused**: Only specific grounds allow a refusal, and some can be reviewed.

> **WARNING**  
> Never send records to someone because they say the patient asked. The client confirms the patient's identity and handles the request.

### 4.5 What is in the designated record set

The **designated record set** is the group of records the clinic uses to make decisions about a patient. It is broader than the medical chart.

**In the set**
- Medical records
- Billing records and claims
- Enrollment and payment records for a health plan
- Records used to make decisions about the patient

**Not open to the right of access**
- Psychotherapy notes
- Information compiled for use in a legal proceeding
- A few other narrow exclusions the client applies

### 4.6 The right to ask for a correction

A patient who believes information in their record is wrong or incomplete may ask in writing for it to be **amended**. The clinic must act within 60 days, with one 30-day extension.

**The clinic may deny when the information**
- Was not created by the clinic
- Is not part of the designated record set
- Is not available for the patient to inspect
- Is already accurate and complete

**The patient can then**
- Submit a written statement of disagreement
- Ask that the request and the denial become part of their record
- File a complaint with the clinic or HHS

> **INFO**  
> You never change a clinical or billing record because a patient says it is wrong. Take the request, and pass it to the client.

### 4.7 The right to an accounting of disclosures

A patient may ask for a list of certain disclosures of their information made in the **six years** before the request. The clinic must respond within 60 days, with one 30-day extension, and one request in a year is free.

The list does not include everything. Disclosures for treatment, payment and operations, to the patient themselves, under an authorization and a few others are left out. What it does include are the kinds of sharing a patient would not expect, like some legal and public-health disclosures.

> **TIP**  
> This is one reason clients keep a record of unusual disclosures, and one reason you never share anything outside the approved way: a disclosure nobody recorded can still be asked about.

### 4.8 The right to ask for a restriction

A patient can ask a clinic to limit how it uses or shares their information, for example not to share a visit with a particular family member. The clinic does not have to agree, but if it does, it must follow the agreement.

> **WARNING: One firm exception**  
> If a patient pays for a service in full out of their own pocket and asks that it not be shared with their health plan, the clinic must honor that for payment and operations purposes, unless the law requires otherwise.

If a restriction has been agreed, the client will tell you how it affects your task. Follow it exactly.

### 4.9 Confidential communications

A patient may ask to be contacted in a particular way or at a particular place: for example, only by mobile phone, not the home landline, or by mail to a different address. Providers must accommodate **reasonable** requests, and cannot require a reason.

**Case: The mailing address**

You send monthly statements for a clinic. A patient calls and says her husband opens her mail, and asks that statements go to her workplace address instead. The record shows a request had been taken, but the address in your mailing list is unchanged.

_Question:_ What should happen before the next statement is sent?

_Answer:_ The statement must not go to the old address. The request is a confidential-communication request, and the clinic must accommodate it if reasonable. Do not guess: ask your lead or client contact to confirm the request is on file and whether the address has been updated, and hold that patient's statement until they confirm.

### 4.10 Personal representatives

A **personal representative** is a person the law allows to act for the patient in health care matters, for example a parent for a young child, a guardian, or someone with a health care power of attorney. In general, the clinic treats them as the patient for those matters.

- Who counts is decided by state law and the client's records, not by what a caller claims.
- Being a relative, a spouse or a friend is not enough by itself.
- There are exceptions, for example where a minor may consent to their own care or where there is concern about abuse.

> **TIP**  
> The question to ask the client is not "is she the mother?" but "is she on file as the patient's personal representative?"

### 4.11 Complaints, and no retaliation

A patient who thinks their rights were not respected may complain to the clinic or to HHS's Office for Civil Rights, generally within 180 days of when they knew about the problem. Clinics must have a process for it, and a contact person.

- A clinic may not retaliate against anyone for filing a complaint or taking part in an investigation.
- A patient cannot be asked to give up these rights as a condition of care.
- Treat a complaint like any request: be kind, take down only the facts, and pass it on.

### 4.12 What you do when a patient asks

The same steps work for every right:

1. **Listen and thank them**: Be kind. Do not say yes, no or "that is not allowed".
2. **Take down only the request**: What they want, how to reach them, and the date. No clinical detail.
3. **Do not act yourself**: Do not send records, change records or make promises.
4. **Pass it on the client's way**: Use the client's form, line or contact, the same day.
5. **Tell the patient what happens next**: That the clinic will respond, and roughly when, if the client has said so.

### 4.13 Try it: the records request

**Scenario: A caller wants her records**

A woman calls the clinic's line. She says she is a patient and wants a copy of her records for a new doctor. She sounds impatient and says the clinic always takes too long.

- Tell her you will email her chart right away to make up for the delay.  
  _You cannot verify her identity or release records. The client handles access requests._
- (best) Thank her, take her request and contact details, tell her the clinic will respond, and pass it to the client's records contact the same day.  
  _Correct. This respects her right and keeps the information with the people who can release it._
- Tell her the clinic does not give out records over the phone and end the call.  
  _Patients do have a right of access. The answer is to route the request, not to refuse it._

### 4.14 Terms from this module

**Terms**
- **Notice of privacy practices**: The clinic's statement of how it uses information and what rights patients have.
- **Designated record set**: The records a clinic uses to make decisions about a patient, including medical and billing records.
- **Amendment**: A correction to a patient's record, requested by the patient.
- **Accounting of disclosures**: A list of certain disclosures of a patient's information, going back six years.
- **Restriction**: A limit on use or sharing that a patient requests, binding if the clinic agrees.
- **Personal representative**: A person the law allows to act for the patient in health care matters.

## Module 5: Business associates and the agreements behind your work

_What a business associate is, what the business associate agreement must contain, who is responsible for what, and why a careless vendor is everyone's problem._

About 8 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials.

Key points:
- A business associate performs functions for a covered entity that involve patient information.
- A business associate agreement is required before any patient information is shared.
- A business associate must bind its own subcontractors to the same limits.
- Since 2009, business associates are directly responsible for the Security Rule and answer to the government.
- A business associate must report a breach to the covered entity without unreasonable delay.
- Tools and vendors you use for client work are part of the chain and must be approved.

### 5.1 Who is a business associate

A **business associate** is a person or company that does something **on behalf of** a covered entity that involves patient information, and is not a member of that entity's own workforce.

- Billing, claims processing and collections
- Data analysis, quality review and practice management
- Legal, accounting, consulting and accreditation services
- IT support, hosting, cloud storage and software that holds patient information
- Administrative support such as scheduling, transcription and virtual assistant services

> **INFO**  
> ERS is a business associate of many of its clients. The tools and vendors ERS uses to do that work can be business associates of ERS, which makes them subcontractors.

### 5.2 No agreement, no information

A covered entity may share patient information with a business associate only after it has received written **satisfactory assurances** that the information will be safeguarded. In practice, that means a signed **business associate agreement**.

The same is true one step down the chain: when a business associate uses a subcontractor, they need an agreement too, containing the same restrictions.

> **WARNING**  
> If you are asked to use a tool that has not been approved, the missing piece may be a contract. That is a reason to ask, even when the tool seems harmless.

### 5.3 What the agreement must contain

A business associate agreement is not free-form. HIPAA lists what it must say. Here is the core, and what each item means for your work:

**Required provisions of a business associate agreement**

| The business associate must... | On the job, this means |
| --- | --- |
| Use and disclose information only as the contract allows | Stay within the task. No side uses. |
| Use appropriate safeguards and follow the Security Rule for electronic information | Approved systems, strong sign-in, locked screens. |
| Report any use or disclosure the contract does not allow, including breaches | Report quickly and truthfully. |
| Make sure subcontractors agree to the same restrictions | Use only approved vendors and tools. |
| Help the client respond to patients' access, amendment and accounting requests | Route requests the same day. |
| Make its records available to HHS for compliance reviews | Keep records as instructed. |
| Return or destroy the information when the contract ends, if feasible | Never keep copies. |

### 5.4 When the agreement is broken

The agreement must let the covered entity **end the contract** if the business associate violates a material term. A client that learns a vendor has been careless is not just upset: the law expects it to act.

1. **The client learns of a problem**: From a report, an audit or a patient complaint.
2. **It must act**: Work with the associate to cure it, or end the contract.
3. **The consequence lands on people**: ERS may lose the client, and the team loses the work.

> **TIP**  
> Fast, honest reporting is how an incident stays a problem to solve instead of a reason to end a contract.

### 5.5 Business associates answer directly

Before 2009, a business associate answered mainly to the covered entity through the contract. Since the HITECH Act and the 2013 Omnibus Rule, business associates and their subcontractors are **directly liable** for complying with the Security Rule and for certain parts of the Privacy Rule.

**Who answers to whom**
- **HHS and the Department of Justice**: Can act directly against covered entities and business associates.
- **The covered entity**: Answers to HHS, and to its patients.
- **The business associate**: Answers to HHS and to the covered entity under the agreement.
- **Subcontractors**: Answer to the business associate, and directly under the rules.

### 5.6 Worked case: the careless vendor

**Case: A scheduling tool without a contract**

A VA team lead finds a scheduling app that is cheaper and easier than the one the client approved. She starts using it for a clinic's appointments, planning to tell the client later. Three months on, the app's database is exposed online and appointment lists, with patient names and visit reasons, can be seen by anyone.

_Question:_ What went wrong, and who is affected?

_Answer:_ The app was used without an agreement, so nothing bound it to safeguard the information, to limit its use or to report the exposure. The clinic never approved it, so it could not assess the risk. The exposure is a breach of unsecured information that the clinic must now handle, patients may have to be told, and ERS and the team lead answer for using an unapproved vendor. A short question at the start would have prevented all of it.

### 5.7 A business associate's duty to tell

When a business associate discovers a breach of unsecured information, it must notify the covered entity **without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days** after discovery. Many contracts require much faster, sometimes within days.

That duty depends on you. A breach is considered discovered when anyone in the workforce knows, or with reasonable care would have known, so a day you wait can use up the client's time.

> **WARNING**  
> Do not wait to be sure. Reporting a possible incident internally the same day is how ERS meets its own deadline.

### 5.8 Where and how you work is part of the contract

Client agreements, and sometimes payer rules, can say where information may be stored and from where work may be done, and which systems may be used. The agreement, not your preference, decides.

- Work only from the places and devices the client and ERS approved.
- Never move client work to your own account, storage or tools.
- If a client's rule seems impractical, raise it with your lead; do not work around it.

### 5.9 Quick check

**Check yourself:** A client's office manager asks you to forward appointment lists to her personal email so she can work at home. What is the best response?

- (correct) Politely decline and ask your lead for the approved way to give her access  
  _Right. Personal email is outside the approved chain, and the office manager's request does not change that._
- Send it, because she is the client  
  _The client's own staff can still break the client's rules. The approved systems are the rule._
- Send it with the names removed  
  _Appointment dates and times can still identify patients, and the channel is still unapproved._

### 5.10 Terms from this module

**Terms**
- **Business associate**: A person or company that does work for a covered entity involving patient information.
- **Business associate agreement**: The written contract that sets how a business associate may use and must protect patient information.
- **Subcontractor**: A business associate's own vendor that handles patient information to help with the work.
- **Satisfactory assurances**: The covered entity's required written promise from the business associate that it will safeguard information.
- **Unsecured PHI**: PHI that is not rendered unusable or unreadable to others, for example by approved encryption.

## Module 6: The Security Rule: protecting electronic patient information

_What the Security Rule protects, how its three kinds of safeguards fit together, what risk analysis is, and how it shapes your workstation, devices, sign-in and daily habits._

About 18 minutes. Included in tracks: full.

Key points:
- The Security Rule protects the confidentiality, integrity and availability of electronic patient information.
- It asks for administrative, physical and technical safeguards, chosen after a risk analysis.
- Addressable does not mean optional: it means implement it, or document why and use an equal alternative.
- Your login is yours alone, and everything done with it is logged.
- Your workstation is part of the client's workplace, wherever it is.
- Use only approved systems, and keep extra copies of patient information from existing.
- A lost or stolen device is an incident: report it immediately.

### 6.1 Why there is a separate Security Rule

The Privacy Rule says who may see information. It does not, by itself, stop someone from stealing a laptop, guessing a password or opening an infected email. The **Security Rule** fills that gap for **electronic** protected health information (ePHI): it requires safeguards so that the allowed uses are the only uses that happen.

> **INFO**  
> The rule is deliberately flexible: it tells organizations what to achieve and lets them choose how, based on their size, their risks and the technology they use.

### 6.2 What security protects

The Security Rule is about keeping **electronic** patient information safe. It protects three things:

1. **Confidentiality**: Only the right people can see it.
2. **Integrity**: It is accurate and has not been changed by mistake or on purpose.
3. **Availability**: The people who need it can get to it when they need it.

> **INFO**  
> A ransomware attack that locks a clinic out of its records is a security incident even if no one reads a single record.

### 6.3 Three kinds of safeguards

The rule asks organizations to use three kinds of safeguards together, on top of the Privacy Rule's own reasonable safeguards for all PHI:

**Safeguards for electronic PHI**
- **Technical**: Unique logins, authentication, encryption, activity logs, automatic log-off.
- **Physical**: Protecting buildings, rooms, workstations and devices.
- **Administrative**: Policies, training, risk analysis, who gets access, how incidents are handled, sanctions.

You are part of all three: you take the training (administrative), protect your workstation and devices (physical), and use the technical tools correctly.

### 6.4 Risk analysis: how the safeguards are chosen

The heart of the rule is a **risk analysis**: finding where ePHI is, what could go wrong with it, and how likely and how serious each risk is. The safeguards then follow the risks, and the work is repeated as things change.

1. **Find the information**: Where is ePHI created, stored, sent and received?
2. **Find the risks**: What threats and weaknesses could expose, change or block it?
3. **Rate them**: How likely, and how harmful?
4. **Act and document**: Put safeguards in place and write down what was decided.
5. **Review again**: When the work, the tools or the threats change.

> **TIP**  
> You help the analysis by reporting what you see: a tool nobody approved, a shared account, a place where patient information piles up.

### 6.5 "Required" and "addressable"

Some parts of the Security Rule are **required**. Others are **addressable**: the organization must put them in place, or document why not and use an equally effective alternative. Addressable does not mean optional.

**Required**
- Must be implemented as written
- Example: a data backup plan
- Example: a risk analysis

**Addressable**
- Implement it, or
- Document why it is not reasonable and appropriate
- And use an equal alternative

> **TIP**  
> For you the result is simple: the controls ERS and your client set up are not optional for you, whatever label the rule gives them.

### 6.6 Administrative safeguards in plain words

**What the rule asks of the organization**

| Standard | In plain words | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Security management | Analyze risks, manage them, apply sanctions, review activity | Sanctions, audit logs |
| Assigned responsibility | Someone is named responsible for security | The Privacy and Security contact |
| Workforce security | Access is authorized, supervised and ended when work ends | Your account and its closing |
| Information access management | People get only the access their role needs | Role-based access |
| Awareness and training | Reminders, malware protection, log-in monitoring, password management | This course |
| Incident procedures | Respond to and report security incidents | Module 7 |
| Contingency plan | Backups, disaster recovery, emergency operation | Why you never delete records |

### 6.7 Physical safeguards and your workspace

The rule requires policies on how a workstation that can reach patient information is **used**, and on the **physical safeguards** around it. Working from home does not lower the standard: for as long as patient information is on your screen, your workspace is part of the client's workplace.

- Work in a space where others cannot see your screen or hear your calls.
- Position your screen away from windows and doorways.
- Keep family, roommates and visitors out of view of patient information.
- Lock your computer whenever you step away, even at home.
- Keep a tidy desk: no notes or printouts with patient details left out.

### 6.8 Approved systems only

Use only the systems, tools and accounts your client and ERS approved. A convenient tool is not an approved tool.

**Approved**
- The client's EHR or portal
- The email and chat your client set up
- Devices and software ERS or the client provided or approved

**Not approved**
- Personal email or messaging apps
- Free file-sharing or notes apps
- AI chatbots and online converters that receive pasted patient information

> **WARNING**  
> Pasting patient information into a public AI tool, translator or converter is a disclosure to an unauthorized party.

### 6.9 Saving, downloading, printing and photographing

The safest place for patient information is where it already is: inside the client's system. Every extra copy is another place it can leak, and another thing the client cannot control.

- Do not save patient information on your own computer unless your client allows it.
- Do not put it in personal cloud storage or notes apps.
- Do not print it at home, or photograph a screen, unless the client has told you how.
- Do not forward it to yourself to "finish later".

> **TIP**  
> If you need to keep something to do your task, ask. The answer may be a safe place the client already has.

### 6.10 Devices and media

The rule has a standard for **device and media controls**: how devices and storage that hold ePHI are received, moved, reused and disposed of. At your desk, that means:

- Use only devices and storage your client or ERS approved.
- Never reuse or give away a device, drive or memory stick that held patient information without it being wiped the approved way.
- Do not take devices from the work area for repair or sale without asking.
- Never leave a work device unattended in a car, a cafe or a shared space.

### 6.11 Lost, stolen or shared devices

A lost or stolen laptop, phone or drive that holds or could reach patient information is a **security incident** from the moment you notice. Encryption can reduce the risk, but whether anyone must be notified is the client's decision.

1. **Report immediately**: Tell your lead and HR right away, even at night.
2. **Say what it could reach**: Which accounts and systems were signed in.
3. **Follow the instructions**: You may be asked to change passwords or sign out sessions.
4. **Do not wait or search first**: Time matters more than recovering the device yourself.

### 6.12 Your login is yours alone

The rule requires every user to have a **unique user identification**, so each action in a system can be tracked to a person. If you share your login, your name is on whatever someone else does.

**Do**
- Use your own unique login
- Keep passwords private
- Use multi-factor sign-in every time it is offered
- Sign out when your work is done

**Do not**
- Share a password with a teammate
- Let anyone use your session
- Write passwords on a sticky note
- Reuse your work password elsewhere

### 6.13 Passwords and multi-factor sign-in

HIPAA's security awareness training includes **password management**: creating, changing and protecting passwords. The rule also requires systems to **verify that a person is who they say they are**.

**Multi-factor authentication (MFA)** adds a second proof, such as a code from an app. The current rule does not name MFA, but ERS and most clients require it, and HHS has proposed making it required.

> **TIP**  
> If a system asks you for a second step, that is the safeguard doing its job. Never share a code with anyone.

### 6.14 Access by role, and logging off

**Access** is limited to what each person's job needs, and it **ends when their work ends**: the rule calls for termination procedures so a former team member's accounts are closed. **Automatic log-off** ends a session after a period of inactivity so a forgotten screen does not stay open.

Do not disable, work around or extend these controls. If they get in the way of your work, tell your lead so the right fix can be made.

**Check yourself:** You notice your login can open another team's patient files. What should you do?

- (correct) Do not open them, and tell your lead  
  _Right. Extra access is a gap to be fixed, not a permission._
- Look around once to see how the system works  
  _Exploring is itself a violation._
- Ignore it and tell nobody  
  _Extra access should be reported so it can be removed._

### 6.15 Everything is logged

Systems keep **audit logs**: who opened which record and when. The rule requires organizations to record and examine this activity. Clients and ERS review it. This is how inappropriate access, curiosity and mistakes are found.

**Check yourself:** Why does it matter that audit logs exist?

- (correct) They record who accessed what, so misuse can be found  
  _Right. Logs are how organizations notice and investigate problems._
- They let you hide your activity  
  _Logs do the opposite._
- They are only for IT staff and do not matter to you  
  _They record your actions too._

### 6.16 Sending information safely

**Transmission security** means information is protected while it travels over a network. Use only the channels your client approved: their secure portal, encrypted email, or the system they set up.

- Do not use personal email, text messages or consumer chat apps for patient information.
- Check the address twice before sending.
- Attach only what is needed, and never more than the minimum.
- Check a fax number or a link before you send, and confirm receipt if your client asks.

### 6.17 Keeping information accurate and available

**Integrity** means protecting information from being changed or destroyed improperly. Be careful when you edit or enter data: a wrong date or a wrong patient can cause real harm to care and billing.

**Availability** means people can reach the information when they need it. Backups and recovery plans exist so it can be restored after a virus, a crash or a disaster, and a data backup plan is a required part of contingency planning. Do not delete records, turn off backups or install tools unless you have been told to. If something stops working, report it.

### 6.18 The Security Rule is being updated

HHS announced a proposal for a stricter Security Rule in December 2024, and it was published for comment in January 2025. Among other things, it would make multi-factor authentication and encryption of electronic patient information required (with limited exceptions), remove the "addressable" category, and require written incident response plans that are tested.

It is a **proposal**. The current rule stays in effect until HHS finishes its work. ERS and its clients already use many of these practices, so your daily habits do not change.

> **TIP**  
> ERS updates this course when the rules change. Always follow the version in front of you and your client's instructions.

### 6.19 Try it: the account that should have closed

**Scenario: A former teammate is still in the system**

A teammate left ERS a month ago. While working in a client's system you notice their account is still active and was used yesterday.

- Ignore it. Someone else will have noticed.  
  _An account that should have ended and is still in use is a serious gap. Report it._
- (best) Do not use or touch it, and tell your lead and HR straight away.  
  _Correct. Access must end with the work, and a possibly misused account is a security incident._
- Use the account yourself to check what it can open.  
  _That would be using someone else's login, which is itself a violation._

### 6.20 Try it: the cafe

**Scenario: The internet is down at home**

Your home internet is out and a client deadline is two hours away. A nearby cafe has free Wi-Fi, and the only seat is at a table beside a window facing the street.

- Work there and keep the screen dim.  
  _People nearby and through the window can still see the screen, and public Wi-Fi is not approved._
- (best) Tell your lead about the outage and ask for the approved way to continue, such as a secure hotspot or a handoff.  
  _Correct. Your lead and client can arrange a safe way, and the deadline is theirs to adjust._
- Work there, but only on tasks with fewer patient names.  
  _Fewer names is still PHI in a place without safeguards._

### 6.21 Terms from this module

**Terms**
- **Security Rule**: The HIPAA rule that requires safeguards for electronic protected health information.
- **Confidentiality, integrity, availability**: The three goals: only the right people see it, it stays accurate, and it can be reached when needed.
- **Risk analysis**: Finding where ePHI is, what could go wrong with it, and how serious each risk is.
- **Addressable**: A specification the organization implements, or documents why not and uses an equal alternative.
- **Unique user identification**: A login that belongs to one person, so every action can be traced.
- **Audit log**: A record of who accessed which information and when.
- **Multi-factor authentication**: Signing in with a second proof, such as a code from an app, in addition to a password.

## Module 7: Privacy and security incidents and breaches

_What counts as an incident, how a breach is decided, who must be told and by when, and exactly what to do and not do when something goes wrong._

About 12 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials, refresher.

Key points:
- An incident is anything that may have exposed patient information or weakened its protection.
- A breach is an impermissible use or disclosure that compromises PHI, and the law presumes it is one unless a risk assessment shows otherwise.
- The client and ERS decide whether an incident is a reportable breach, not the person who found it.
- Notification deadlines run from the day the incident is known, so report the same day.
- Do not investigate, fix quietly, delete evidence or contact the patient yourself.
- Good-faith reporting is protected, and nobody may retaliate against someone who files a complaint.

### 7.1 What is an incident?

An **incident** is anything that may have exposed, changed or blocked access to patient information, or that breaks a privacy or security rule.

- A message with patient information sent to the wrong person
- A lost or stolen laptop or phone
- A clicked phishing link or a shared password
- Records opened without a work reason
- A system that locks up or shows data that is not yours

> **INFO**  
> You do not have to be sure it is serious. If you wonder whether it is an incident, report it.

### 7.2 What usually goes wrong

Most incidents are ordinary mistakes, not attacks. These come up again and again:

- Patient information sent or faxed to the wrong person
- A laptop, phone or drive lost or stolen
- Records opened without a work reason
- A shared or borrowed login
- Paper or old devices thrown away without being destroyed
- A phishing message, virus or other attack
- Missing training or missing agreements with vendors

### 7.3 Incident and breach

A **breach** is an impermissible **acquisition, access, use or disclosure** of PHI that compromises its privacy or security. Not every incident is a breach, and that decision is not yours to make.

**Incident**
- Anything that may have exposed or weakened protection
- Reported by anyone, the same day
- Often turns out to be low risk

**Breach**
- An impermissible acquisition, access, use or disclosure that compromises PHI
- Decided by the client and ERS after an assessment
- Triggers notification duties

> **TIP**  
> Your job is to report quickly and truthfully. Theirs is to assess and decide.

### 7.4 Presumed to be a breach

The law starts from the opposite of what people expect. An impermissible use or disclosure is **presumed to be a breach** unless the organization shows, through a documented risk assessment, that there is a **low probability the information was compromised**.

The burden is on the organization. That is why a quick, accurate report matters: it gives them the facts to meet that burden.

### 7.5 The four-factor risk assessment

To decide whether information was compromised, the client and ERS assess at least four things:

**What the assessment considers**

| Factor | The question | A reassuring answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The information | How sensitive was it, and could it be tied to a person? | A few non-identifying details |
| The recipient | Who got it or saw it? | Another HIPAA-covered organization |
| Acquired or viewed | Was it actually opened or taken? | Never opened |
| Mitigation | Has the risk been reduced? | Deleted with written confirmation |

> **INFO**  
> You supply the facts for these four: what was sent, to whom, whether it was opened and what has been done. That is why your report should be factual and complete.

### 7.6 Three situations that are not breaches

The rule excludes three narrow situations from the definition of a breach:

- **Good-faith, unintentional access** by a workforce member, within their authority, with no further impermissible use.
- **Inadvertent sharing between two authorized people** at the same organization or arrangement, with no further impermissible use.
- **A good-faith belief that the recipient could not keep the information**, for example it was returned unopened.

> **WARNING**  
> These are for the client and ERS to apply. Never decide an exception applies and keep quiet. Report it, and let them decide.

### 7.7 Secured and unsecured PHI

The notification rules apply to **unsecured PHI**: information that has not been made unusable, unreadable or indecipherable to unauthorized people. The government's guidance recognizes two ways to secure it:

**Encryption**
- Electronic data made unreadable without a key
- The key is not stored with the data
- Meets recognized standards

**Destruction**
- Paper shredded or burned so it cannot be rebuilt
- Electronic media cleared, purged or destroyed
- Not just deleted or thrown away

> **TIP**  
> A properly encrypted laptop that is stolen is generally not a reportable breach. But whether it was properly encrypted is for the client and ERS to confirm, never you. Report every loss.

### 7.8 Who is told, and by when

When a breach is confirmed, the covered entity has several notification duties, all with the same outer limit of **60 days** from discovery, and a duty to act without unreasonable delay within it.

**After a breach is found**
1. **Day 0**: Anyone in the workforce knows, or with reasonable care would have known. The clock starts.
2. **Right away**: ERS tells the client, often within days under the contract.
3. **Within 60 days**: The client tells each affected person, in writing.
4. **Within 60 days, 500 or more people**: The client also tells HHS and, for large breaches in one state, prominent media.
5. **After the year ends**: Smaller breaches are reported to HHS within 60 days after the end of the calendar year.

### 7.9 What a notice to patients says

A breach notice is written in plain language and includes: what happened and when it happened and was found; the types of information involved; what patients should do to protect themselves; what the organization is doing; and how to contact it.

> **INFO**  
> It is written by the client, using the facts you and ERS gave. You never contact a patient about an incident yourself.

### 7.10 What to do

1. **Stop**: Stop what you are doing with the information. Do not try to undo it yourself.
2. **Tell**: Report to your team lead and HR immediately, and follow your client's instructions.
3. **Describe**: Say what happened, when, and which system. Leave out patient names and details.
4. **Preserve**: Keep the message, file or device as it is. Do not delete anything.
5. **Follow instructions**: Do what you are asked, and nothing more.

### 7.11 What not to do

**Do**
- Report right away
- Write facts only
- Keep evidence
- Cooperate fully

**Do not**
- Wait and see
- Fix it quietly
- Delete or edit records
- Contact the patient or discuss it with others

> **WARNING**  
> Trying to hide a mistake is far more serious than the mistake. Honest and fast is always the right answer.

### 7.12 Worked case: the right name, the wrong address

**Case: A schedule to the wrong clinic**

At 4:50 pm you send a next-day schedule, with patient names and appointment times, to a clinic contact. At 5:05 pm you notice your email program filled in a different clinic's contact with the same first name. The message was delivered and the other contact has not replied.

_Question:_ What do you do, in what order, and what do you leave out of your report?

_Answer:_ Stop, and do not send a second email asking them to ignore the first. Tell your lead and HR at once, and use the client's incident steps. Describe the facts: what was sent, to which address, at what time, whether it was opened if you know, and that you have not tried to fix it. Leave out the patient names and the schedule contents. Keep the sent message as it is. The client and ERS will assess it, and ask the other clinic to confirm deletion if that is the right step.

### 7.13 You are protected

HIPAA prohibits retaliating against someone for filing a complaint about a possible violation or taking part in an investigation. ERS goes further: it does not punish anyone for reporting a possible incident in good faith. It does apply sanctions for deliberate violations and for hiding incidents.

**Check yourself:** You made an honest mistake and sent a message to the wrong client contact. What is the best next step?

- (correct) Report it immediately to your lead and HR  
  _Right. Fast, honest reporting is protected and limits harm._
- Wait to see if the person replies  
  _Waiting uses up time the client may need._
- Ask the person to delete it and say nothing  
  _A request is not a fix. The incident must still be reported._

### 7.14 Try it: the wrong recipient

**Scenario: It went to the wrong person**

You email a schedule with patient names to a clinic contact. A minute later you notice the address autofilled to a similarly named person at a different company.

- Send a second email asking them to ignore the first and move on.  
  _That does not report the incident, and it creates another message._
- (best) Tell your lead and HR immediately, describe what you sent and where, and follow the client's instructions.  
  _Correct. The earlier it is reported, the more can be done._
- Hope the person does not open it, and fix your autofill.  
  _Hoping is not a response, and the incident would stay hidden._

### 7.15 Terms from this module

**Terms**
- **Incident**: Anything that may have exposed, changed or blocked access to information, or broken a rule.
- **Breach**: An impermissible acquisition, access, use or disclosure of PHI that compromises its privacy or security.
- **Unsecured PHI**: PHI that has not been made unusable, unreadable or indecipherable to unauthorized people.
- **Risk assessment**: The documented review of four factors used to decide whether PHI was compromised.
- **Discovery**: The first day a breach is known, or would have been known with reasonable care.
- **Retaliation**: Punishing someone for filing a complaint or taking part in an investigation, which HIPAA prohibits.

## Module 8: Enforcement, penalties and real-world cases

_How a complaint becomes an investigation, what the civil and criminal penalty tiers mean, three generic cases that show how problems actually arise, and the other laws that sit beside HIPAA._

About 8 minutes. Included in tracks: full.

Key points:
- OCR investigates complaints and reports, and can resolve them with guidance, an agreement or a penalty.
- Civil penalties follow four tiers, from not knowing to willful neglect that was not corrected.
- Knowingly misusing patient information is a crime, with more serious penalties for false pretenses and for gain or harm.
- State attorneys general can also enforce HIPAA, and states have their own laws.
- Real cases usually come from ordinary failures: no risk analysis, an unprotected device, an unapproved vendor.
- Other laws, such as rules for substance use disorder records, can add to HIPAA.

### 8.1 Who enforces HIPAA

Several bodies can act when HIPAA is not followed:


| Who | What they do |
| --- | --- |
| HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) | Investigates complaints and breach reports; resolves cases with guidance, agreements or penalties |
| Department of Justice | Prosecutes criminal violations |
| State attorneys general | Can bring civil actions on behalf of state residents |
| The client and ERS | Apply contract remedies and sanctions |

### 8.2 How a complaint becomes a case

1. **A complaint or a report**: A patient, a worker or a breach report brings the matter to OCR.
2. **Review**: OCR decides whether HIPAA applies and whether the facts show a possible violation.
3. **Investigation**: OCR asks for policies, risk analyses, training records, logs and an explanation.
4. **Resolution**: Guidance, a voluntary fix, a resolution agreement with a corrective plan and a payment, or a civil money penalty.

> **INFO**  
> Most cases end before a penalty. What helps is evidence of good faith: training records, a risk analysis, quick reporting and a fix.

### 8.3 The four civil penalty tiers

Civil penalties scale with how much the organization knew and what it did next. The dollar limits are adjusted for inflation each year, so this course describes the tiers, not the amounts.

**From least to most serious**
- **Tier 1: did not know**: The organization did not know, and with reasonable diligence would not have known, of the violation.
- **Tier 2: reasonable cause**: A violation the organization should have known about, but not through willful neglect.
- **Tier 3: willful neglect, corrected**: Conscious disregard of the rules, fixed within 30 days of finding out.
- **Tier 4: willful neglect, not corrected**: Conscious disregard of the rules, and not fixed within 30 days.

> **TIP**  
> Fixing a problem quickly moves a violation toward a lower tier. That is one more reason to report the same day.

### 8.4 Criminal penalties

A person who **knowingly** obtains or discloses identifiable health information in violation of the rules can be prosecuted. The Department of Justice handles these cases, and the penalties rise with intent:


| The conduct | Maximum prison term |
| --- | --- |
| Knowingly obtaining or disclosing health information | 1 year |
| The same, under false pretenses | 5 years |
| With intent to sell, transfer or use for commercial advantage, personal gain or malicious harm | 10 years |

> **WARNING**  
> These apply to individuals, not only organizations. Looking up information to sell it, to help a friend or to hurt someone is not a mistake: it is the conduct the law is aimed at.

### 8.5 Case one: the curious employee

**Case: Looking without a reason**

An employee at a large healthcare provider opened the records of several patients who were not part of her work, including a coworker and an acquaintance who had been in the news. Nobody was told and she did not share anything. An audit of the access logs found it months later.

_Question:_ Was this a violation, even though she told no one and used no information?

_Answer:_ Yes. Opening records without a work reason is an impermissible use. Access logs recorded each look, and an audit surfaced them. The consequences can include the employee's dismissal, a breach analysis by the organization, notification to the patients involved, and in cases involving intent or false pretenses, criminal exposure. The lesson is the first rule of this course: access is not permission.

### 8.6 Case two: the missing risk analysis

**Case: A stolen laptop, and what came after**

A small practice's unencrypted laptop, holding records for thousands of patients, was stolen from a car. During the investigation OCR asked for the practice's most recent security risk analysis. There was none, and no policy covered encryption or portable devices.

_Question:_ What made this a case, and what would have changed the outcome?

_Answer:_ The theft started it, but the case grew because the practice could not show a risk analysis or safeguards for portable devices. The resolution included a payment and a multi-year corrective plan. A documented risk analysis, encryption on the device and a clear policy would not have prevented the theft, but they would have prevented the breach and the finding that safeguards were absent.

### 8.7 Case three: the testimonial

**Case: A thank-you on the website**

A small clinic posted patient photos and short thank-you stories on its website and social pages. The patients were happy and had said yes in person, but no signed authorizations were on file. A patient later complained to OCR.

_Question:_ Why was this a problem, and who would have caught it?

_Answer:_ Using a patient's photo or story to promote the practice needs the patient's written authorization, and saying yes in person is not enough. The clinic had to remove the content and take corrective steps. The person who maintained the website was in the best position to ask: "Is there a signed authorization on file for each of these?"

### 8.8 State laws sit beside HIPAA

HIPAA is a federal floor. States can add to it with their own privacy, breach-notification and medical-records laws, and state attorneys general can enforce HIPAA itself. When a state law is more protective, the stricter rule applies.

### 8.9 Other federal laws that touch the same information

Some information sits under another law as well as HIPAA, or instead of it. You do not need the details. You need to know that the client's instructions already reflect them:


| Law or rule | What it adds |
| --- | --- |
| Substance use disorder treatment records (42 CFR Part 2) | Extra limits on sharing records from certain treatment programs |
| Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) | Limits on using genetic information in insurance and employment |
| Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) | Governs student education records, which are not PHI |
| State laws | Sensitive categories, minors, and breach notification |

> **TIP**  
> If a record looks unusual or comes from a specialized program, stop and ask. The extra rule may change who may see it.

### 8.10 Quick check

**Check yourself:** Which action by a clinic usually helps most when OCR investigates?

- (correct) Showing documented training, a risk analysis and quick reporting and fixing  
  _Right. Evidence of good-faith compliance and prompt correction carries weight._
- Saying nobody was hurt  
  _Harm is not the test. The question is whether required safeguards and steps were in place._
- Deleting old logs  
  _Destroying records makes things much worse._

### 8.11 Terms from this module

**Terms**
- **Resolution agreement**: A settlement with OCR that usually includes a payment and a corrective action plan.
- **Corrective action plan**: A set of steps, with monitoring, that an organization agrees to take to fix its compliance.
- **Willful neglect**: Conscious, intentional failure or reckless indifference to the duty to comply.
- **Civil money penalty**: A fine imposed by OCR on a covered entity or business associate.
- **State attorney general**: A state's chief legal officer, who can bring civil actions to enforce HIPAA.

## Module 9: Lab: front desk, scheduling, marketing and websites

_Practice the rules on work that faces patients and the public: calls, reminders, intake, reviews, social posts and website tools._

About 9 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials.

Key points:
- On the phone, confirm who you are speaking with and share only what the client's process allows.
- Reminders and messages carry the minimum: no reason for the visit, no details.
- Never confirm that someone is a patient, publicly or privately, unless the client's process says so.
- A patient's photo, story or review needs a signed authorization before it is used to promote.
- Website forms, scripts and trackers are part of the chain: add none without approval.

### 9.1 How the lab works

Each exercise is a situation from front desk, scheduling, marketing or website work. Decide what you would do before reading the feedback. There are no tricks: every answer comes from the rules you have already learned.

> **TIP**  
> When you are stuck, return to three questions: Is it the minimum? Is it the approved way? Do I know who I am talking to?

### 9.2 Scenario 1: the neighbor on the line

**Scenario: Is she a patient there?**

You answer the clinic line for the client. A man says, "My neighbor Mrs. Alvarez told me she sees Dr. Reyes at your clinic. I want to send her flowers. Can you tell me if she is a patient and when her next visit is?"

- Tell him her next visit, since he is only sending flowers.  
  _Even a visit date is PHI, and the caller is not verified or authorized._
- (best) Say you cannot confirm or share anything about anyone, and offer to take a message for her in case she is a patient.  
  _Correct. You neither confirm nor deny, and the message route lets her decide._
- Say you cannot give the visit date but confirm she is a patient.  
  _Confirming that someone is a patient is itself a disclosure._

### 9.3 Worked case: the reminder voicemail

**Case: What does the voicemail say?**

You make appointment reminder calls for a clinic. A patient's phone goes to a voicemail greeting that is only a robotic voice reading the number. You are about to leave this message: "Hello Mr. Santos, this is Bright Valley Fertility Clinic calling to remind you of your IVF consultation on Thursday at 2 pm."

_Question:_ What should change before you leave the message?

_Answer:_ Use the client's approved voicemail script, which normally gives only what is needed to call back: the clinic's name or a general name, a callback number and, if the client allows, the time of an appointment. Do not name the department or the reason for the visit, since anyone who hears the message learns something about the patient's health. If the client has no script, ask your lead before leaving any message.

### 9.4 Scenario 2: the photo of the form

**Scenario: A patient texts her intake form**

A patient, unable to use the clinic's portal, takes a photo of her completed intake form and sends it from her phone to the clinic's regular text line that you monitor. It includes her insurance number and a medical history.

- Transcribe it into the system from your own phone, then delete the text.  
  _Your phone is not an approved device and the photo has already left the approved path._
- (best) Do not forward or save it. Report it through the client's process and offer her the approved way to submit the form.  
  _Correct. The patient chose the route; you protect the information and report the gap._
- Forward the photo to the clinic manager's personal email so she can see it.  
  _That adds another unapproved channel._

### 9.5 Worked case: the online review

**Case: Replying to a bad review**

You manage a clinic's online reviews. A reviewer writes: "I waited two hours and the nurse was rude when I asked about my results." The practice owner asks you to post a reply right away: "We are sorry. Our records show you were seen at 9 am for a follow-up on your test results and left at 11."

_Question:_ Can you post that reply?

_Answer:_ No. The reply confirms the person was a patient and reveals details of the visit, which is a disclosure. A review being public does not give the clinic permission to respond with patient information. A safe reply says only that the clinic takes feedback seriously and invites the person to contact the office directly, without confirming that they are a patient. Draft that and let the client approve it.

### 9.6 Scenario 3: the grateful patient

**Scenario: A thank-you note for the newsletter**

A patient emails the clinic: "Dr. Reyes changed my life. Please share my story and my photo." The office manager asks you to add it to this month's newsletter and the clinic's social page today.

- Post it. The patient asked for it, and the email is on file.  
  _A casual email is not the signed authorization the rule calls for. The client must have the proper form._
- (best) Ask the client to confirm there is a signed authorization on file covering this use, and hold the post until they do.  
  _Correct. The patient's wish is the start; the signed form is what makes it allowed._
- Post only the first name and a cropped photo.  
  _A cropped photo and a first name can still identify a person. The authorization is the question._

### 9.7 Scenario 4: the analytics tool

**Scenario: Adding a tracker to the booking page**

You are updating a clinic's website. A marketing contact suggests adding a free tracking script and an advertising pixel to the booking and patient login pages so the clinic can see which ads work.

- Add them, because every website does this.  
  _Tools on pages where patients book or sign in can send visit and identity information to the tool's company, without an agreement or the patient's authorization._
- (best) Do not add them. Raise it with your lead and the client, who decide what tools may run on pages that handle patient information, and under what agreement.  
  _Correct. These tools are part of the chain and must be approved first._
- Add them only on the booking page, not the login page.  
  _The booking page also collects patient information. The same approval is needed._

### 9.8 Before you publish or send

Work that goes out to the public cannot be recalled. Run this list every time:

1. **Is any patient in it?**: A name, a photo, a story, a review, a date or a detail that could point to one.
2. **Is there a signed authorization?**: The client confirms it, for this exact use.
3. **Does it confirm someone is a patient?**: Even without a name, or in a reply.
4. **Does any tool see visitors?**: Forms, scripts, trackers and plug-ins need approval and an agreement.
5. **Has the client approved it?**: The client signs off on anything public.

### 9.9 Check yourself

**Check yourself:** A patient asks you to leave a reminder on her husband's phone because hers is broken. What do you do?

- (correct) Do not leave details; take a message for the clinic and follow its process  
  _Right. A spouse is not automatically an authorized recipient, and the client decides._
- Leave the full appointment details, since they are married  
  _Marriage does not make someone an authorized recipient for health information._
- Leave only the time and the doctor's name  
  _That still reveals health care information to someone who may not be authorized._

## Module 10: Lab: billing, insurance and records work

_Practice the rules on billing, insurance verification, collections, faxing, chart preparation and exports._

About 8 minutes. Included in tracks: full.

Key points:
- Payment work is permitted, but only for the minimum information the step needs.
- A collection agency or billing vendor is a business associate and needs an agreement.
- Faxes, exports and shared screens carry patient information and need the same checks as email.
- Every extra copy, spreadsheet or export is a new place for information to leak.
- Check the number, the recipient and the file before you send or share.

### 10.1 How this lab works

These exercises come from billing, insurance and records work. They are harder than the last lab, because here the information is exactly what the job needs, and the risk is in what you do with it next.

> **TIP**  
> For each exercise ask: what is the minimum, where does this copy go, and who exactly will receive it?

### 10.2 Scenario 1: the eligibility check

**Scenario: A payer asks for more**

You call a patient's insurer to verify coverage before an appointment. The representative asks you to also describe the patient's diagnosis and the planned procedure "to confirm benefits". Your task is only to check eligibility.

- Give the diagnosis and procedure, since the insurer is a health plan.  
  _Payment is permitted, but only the minimum for the step. Your task did not require it._
- (best) Give only what the eligibility check needs, and ask your lead or the client if the insurer insists on more.  
  _Correct. Payment is allowed but limited to the minimum necessary._
- Hang up and mark the check as failed.  
  _You can finish the check with the minimum. Hanging up leaves the task undone._

### 10.3 Worked case: the collection agency

**Case: Sending overdue balances out**

A clinic's office manager asks you to send a spreadsheet of overdue patient balances to a collection agency she found online. The sheet has names, addresses, dates of birth, balances and the procedure name for each account. She says it is fine because it is a payment matter.

_Question:_ Is it fine, and what changes before anything is sent?

_Answer:_ Collections are part of payment, so the purpose can be permitted, but two things are missing. First, the agency is a business associate and must have a signed agreement with the clinic before receiving any information. Second, minimum necessary applies: the procedure name is rarely needed, and the sheet should hold only what the agency requires. Raise both with the client, and do not send until there is an agreement and a trimmed file.

### 10.4 Scenario 2: the fax number

**Scenario: A prior authorization by fax**

You need to fax a prior authorization form with a patient's details to a payer. The number on the form is smudged, and a similar number appears in a previous email from a different insurer.

- Guess from the similar number and send it.  
  _A wrong number sends patient information to a stranger._
- (best) Confirm the number from a reliable source, send a cover sheet only first if the client's process allows, and ask for confirmation of receipt.  
  _Correct. Check the destination before the information leaves._
- Email the form to the insurer's general address instead.  
  _That changes to an unapproved channel without confirming the destination._

### 10.5 Scenario 3: the export for the client

**Scenario: A spreadsheet of everyone**

A clinic manager asks for a list of all patients with an unpaid balance, to review during a video call. The billing system can export every field. You are about to export all columns so you will not have to do it again.

- Export every field and send it. It saves time.  
  _That breaks minimum necessary and creates a large file that can leak._
- (best) Export only the columns the review needs, send it through the approved channel, and delete your working copy as the client instructs.  
  _Correct. Limit the columns, use the approved channel and do not keep copies._
- Export everything but send it in two emails.  
  _Splitting the message does not reduce the information shared._

### 10.6 Worked case: the shared screen

**Case: Helping a colleague on a video call**

You are on a video call with a clinic colleague and a billing consultant the clinic does not use regularly. To explain a posting error, the colleague shares her screen with the open patient ledger, including names and balances, and the consultant has not signed anything.

_Question:_ What should happen, and what should you do?

_Answer:_ The consultant may be an outside party with no agreement, so the screen share may be an impermissible disclosure. Ask politely to stop sharing, and suggest using a sample record with fake data. Then tell your lead what happened and what was visible. The client decides whether it is an incident that needs assessment.

### 10.7 Scenario 4: the helpful tool

**Scenario: A faster way to transcribe**

You are given dictated clinic notes to type up. A free browser tool turns audio into text in seconds. The client's approved transcription tool is slow and expensive to scale.

- Use the free tool for the long files only.  
  _Any file would still go to an unapproved company, and length does not change that._
- (best) Use only the approved tool, and raise the speed problem with your lead so the client can decide on an alternative.  
  _Correct. The client decides what tool is approved, and it must be under an agreement._
- Use the free tool after removing the patient's name from the audio.  
  _Voices and content can identify a person, and the tool is still unapproved._

### 10.8 Before you send, share or export

1. **Recipient**: Who exactly, and is there an agreement or a verified authority?
2. **Destination**: Is the fax number, address or system confirmed from a reliable source?
3. **Content**: Only the fields and records the step needs.
4. **Channel**: The approved portal, secure email or system.
5. **Copies**: No extra copies kept, and the working file removed as instructed.

### 10.9 Check yourself

**Check yourself:** You prepare a patient's chart for a new specialist. Which action is the best practice?

- (correct) Send only the sections the specialist's request names, through the approved channel  
  _Right. A treatment disclosure is allowed, and it goes by the approved route with only what is requested._
- Send the entire chart to avoid a second request  
  _Minimum necessary points the other way, and the request names what is needed._
