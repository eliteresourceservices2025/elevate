## Module 1: HIPAA foundations and your role at ERS

_What HIPAA is, who it applies to, where ERS and you fit, and the one habit that keeps you safe: when unsure, stop and ask._

About 13 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials, refresher.

Key points:
- HIPAA protects the privacy and security of people's health information.
- ERS works for clients who are covered by HIPAA, and ERS and its team follow the same rules through contracts called business associate agreements.
- Being able to open information is not the same as being allowed to use it.
- Use or share only what your job and your client's rules allow.
- When you are not sure, stop, do not share, and ask your lead.
- ERS runs a privacy and security program, and training is one required part of it.
- Every organization in the chain from the clinic to ERS is bound by the same agreement, and so are you.

### 1.1 Why this training matters

Many ERS clients are doctors' offices, clinics, billing companies and other healthcare organizations. When you work for them, you may see information about real patients: who they are, what is wrong with them, and what they owe.

That information belongs to the patient. A law called **HIPAA** says how it must be protected, and it applies to you as soon as you start work.

> **INFO: Good news**  
> You do not need to be a lawyer. You need a few clear habits, and this course teaches them in about 80 minutes. Your progress is saved, so you can stop and come back.

### 1.2 What HIPAA is

HIPAA is the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, a United States federal law from 1996. The parts that matter most to you are three rules:

1. **Privacy Rule**: Who may use or share health information, and when.
2. **Security Rule**: How electronic health information must be kept safe.
3. **Breach Notification Rule**: What must happen when health information is exposed.

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) enforces HIPAA through its Office for Civil Rights. Even though ERS is in the Philippines, the work you do for U.S. clients falls under these rules through the contracts ERS signs.

### 1.3 Who is who

HIPAA uses a few labels. Here they are in plain words:

- **Covered entity**: a health plan, a healthcare clearinghouse, or a healthcare provider that bills electronically. Most of our clients are covered entities.
- **Business associate**: a company that does work for a covered entity and handles patient information while doing it.
- **Subcontractor**: a company that a business associate uses to do part of that work.
- **Workforce member**: people whose work is under the organization's direct control, paid by it or not, including contractors like you.

> **TIP**  
> Depending on the client, ERS is a business associate or a subcontractor. Either way, the same protections follow the information to you.

### 1.4 The chain of trust

A clinic cannot simply hand patient information to a vendor. The law builds a chain: every organization that handles the information signs an agreement promising to protect it, and must make the next organization in the chain promise the same.

1. **The clinic or plan**: The covered entity that first holds the information.
2. **ERS**: A business associate, bound by its agreement with the client.
3. **Anyone ERS uses**: Subcontractors, tools and vendors, bound by the same promise.
4. **You**: A member of ERS's workforce, bound by ERS's rules.

> **INFO**  
> The chain only holds if every link does. A rule your client gives you is not the client's own preference: it is the promise that came down the chain, and you are the last link.

### 1.5 Where ERS and you fit

Picture a chain. The patient trusts the clinic. The clinic trusts a company like ERS to help. ERS trusts you to handle the information carefully.

1. **Patient**: Trusts the healthcare provider with their information.
2. **Client**: The provider or organization that hires ERS.
3. **ERS**: Signs a business associate agreement with the client.
4. **You**: Follow ERS's rules and your client's rules every day.

Each client also has its own approved systems, tools and instructions. **Client rules and ERS rules both apply. If they differ, follow the stricter one.**

### 1.6 Access is not permission

Your login may open a whole patient list. That does not mean you may look at every record. You may use information only for the task you were given.

**Allowed**
- Opening a record to complete the task your client assigned
- Using the approved system the client set up
- Sharing only with people your client named for that task

**Not allowed**
- Looking up a neighbor, friend or celebrity
- Copying records to your own phone or email
- Telling anyone about a patient, even "just a little"

> **WARNING**  
> Looking at records out of curiosity is a privacy violation, even if you never tell anyone and even if the system let you in.

### 1.7 The agreement sets the rules

ERS and each client sign a **business associate agreement (BAA)**. It says what ERS may do with patient information, what safeguards are required, and how problems must be reported.

You do not need to read the contract. You do need to follow the instructions your lead and your client give you, because they come from it.

**Check yourself:** Where do you find out what you may do with a client's patient information?

- (correct) In your client's instructions and ERS's rules, as explained by your lead  
  _Right. Those are the rules that apply to your daily work._
- Whatever the system lets you open  
  _Being able to open something does not make it allowed._
- Whatever a coworker says is usually fine  
  _Habits spread, but they are not rules. Ask your lead._

### 1.8 What the agreement asks of ERS

A business associate agreement is specific. Among other things, it requires the business associate to:

- Use patient information only for the purposes the contract allows
- Protect it with appropriate safeguards, including for electronic information
- Report any use or disclosure the contract does not allow, including breaches
- Make sure its own subcontractors agree to the same limits
- Help the client respond to patients' requests to see or correct their records
- Open its records to HHS when asked, and return or destroy the information when the contract ends

> **TIP**  
> Each item is something you do in a normal day: stay within the task, use approved tools, report problems, never keep copies.

### 1.9 How ERS keeps this program running

Following HIPAA is not only about individuals. Organizations must run a privacy and security program, and ERS does. You will see its parts in your daily work:

- **A named privacy contact**: the Privacy Officer, who owns the rules and answers questions.
- **Written policies and agreements**: ERS's policies, and each client's business associate agreement.
- **Risk checks**: regular reviews of where information could be exposed, and fixes.
- **Training**: HIPAA requires every workforce member to be trained, and ERS repeats it every year.
- **Sanctions and reporting**: consequences for breaking the rules, and a way to report problems.
- **Records**: ERS keeps its policy and training records for six years.

> **TIP**  
> You are not expected to run the program. Your part is to follow it, ask when unsure, and report problems.

### 1.10 How HIPAA grew up

HIPAA did not arrive all at once. Knowing the steps explains why ERS, a business associate, is held to the rules directly:

1. **1996**: HIPAA becomes federal law.
2. **2003**: Organizations must comply with the Privacy Rule, which governs how information is used and shared.
3. **2009**: The HITECH Act makes business associates directly responsible for the Security Rule and strengthens enforcement.
4. **2013**: The Omnibus Rule folds HITECH into the HIPAA rules and adds detail on business associates and breach notification.

> **INFO**  
> Since 2009, ERS answers to the government for its own compliance, not only to its clients.

### 1.11 Why it matters to you

Mistakes happen, and ERS wants to hear about them quickly (more on that in Module 6). Deliberately misusing information is different.

- Civil penalties are enforced by HHS's Office for Civil Rights and can fall on organizations, and in some cases on individuals.
- Knowingly misusing patient information is a crime the Department of Justice prosecutes: up to 1 year in prison, 5 with false pretenses, 10 if for gain or harm.
- ERS has sanctions for breaking its privacy and security rules, up to ending your engagement.
- Clients can end their contract with ERS, which affects every teammate.

> **INFO**  
> ERS does not punish good-faith reports, and HIPAA prohibits retaliating against people who file a complaint about a possible violation. Speak up.

### 1.12 Try it: the familiar name

**Scenario: A name you know**

While doing your assigned task you see a patient name that matches your cousin's. Your login shows the full chart, and nobody would ever know.

- Open the chart to check whether it is really your cousin.  
  _That would be curiosity, not your task. Looking is a violation even if nobody finds out._
- (best) Finish only the task you were assigned and do not open anything extra.  
  _Correct. Use only what the task needs._
- Mention it to a friend so they can check on your cousin.  
  _Telling anyone about a patient is a disclosure. It is never okay._

### 1.13 The safest default

You will sometimes meet a situation nobody trained you for. There is one move that is always right:

1. **Stop**: Do not send, open, copy or say anything yet.
2. **Do not share**: Keep the information where it is.
3. **Ask**: Ask your team lead or your client contact for the approved way.

> **TIP**  
> Asking is never a mistake. A question costs a few minutes. A wrong disclosure cannot be taken back.

## Module 2: PHI, ePHI and identifiers

_How to recognize protected health information in any form, including the small details that identify a person._

About 10 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials, refresher.

Key points:
- PHI is health information that can identify a person, in any form: screen, paper, spoken or electronic.
- A name is only one identifier. Dates, numbers, contacts, images and device details can identify someone too.
- Context matters: ordinary details become PHI when they are tied to healthcare.
- Screenshots, exports and copies carry the same protection as the original.
- Removing a name does not make information anonymous.

### 2.1 What is PHI?

**PHI** means protected health information. It is information about a person's health, their healthcare, or payment for healthcare, that identifies them or could be used to identify them.

- Health: a diagnosis, a symptom, a test result, a medication.
- Healthcare: an appointment, a visit, a referral, a procedure.
- Payment: a bill, an insurance claim, a balance owed.

> **INFO**  
> PHI can be on a screen, on paper, in a phone call, or in a conversation. The form does not change the protection.

### 2.2 ePHI: electronic PHI

**ePHI** is PHI that is created, stored, sent or received electronically: an EHR record, an email, a spreadsheet, a scan, a chat message, a screenshot, a voicemail file.

The HIPAA Security Rule is about ePHI. Most of your work will touch ePHI, which is why the safeguards in the next modules matter every day.

**Check yourself:** A PDF of a patient's lab results saved on a computer is:

- (correct) ePHI  
  _Right. It is health information that identifies a person, stored electronically._
- Not protected because it is a file  
  _Files are exactly where ePHI lives._

### 2.3 What identifies a person

HIPAA lists 18 kinds of identifiers. You do not need to memorize a list. Learn the five families:

- **Identity**: name, photo of a face, biometric prints.
- **Contact and place**: any place smaller than a state (street address, city, ZIP code), phone, fax, email.
- **Dates and ages**: birth date, admission or discharge date, appointment date, age over 89.
- **Numbers**: Social Security number, medical record number, insurance or account number, license numbers.
- **Digital and device**: IP address, website address, device serial numbers, vehicle plate numbers.

### 2.4 Context changes the answer

A first name on its own is not much. The same name in a clinic's appointment list is PHI.

**By itself**
- "Maria"
- A phone number on a business card
- "Tuesday 2 PM"

**With healthcare context**
- "Maria, cardiology follow-up"
- A phone number in a patient's chart
- "Tuesday 2 PM, Dr. Reyes, oncology"

> **TIP**  
> Ask yourself: could this tell someone who a patient is, or something about their health or care? If yes, treat it as PHI.

### 2.5 PHI is not only in the EHR

Patient information turns up in places people forget:

- Emails and chat messages with a patient's name in the subject or body
- Scheduling and billing spreadsheets
- Screenshots sent "just to show the problem"
- Sticky notes, printouts and notebooks on a desk
- Voicemail, call recordings and what you say aloud at home
- File names such as "Lopez_MRI_results.pdf"

> **WARNING**  
> Screenshots and copies inherit the sensitivity of what is in them. A screenshot of a chart is PHI.

### 2.6 Removing a name is not enough

Information is only free of HIPAA rules when it is properly **de-identified**: either all 18 identifier types are removed, or an expert formally confirms the risk of identifying anyone is very small.

Deleting the name column from a spreadsheet does not do that. A birth date, a ZIP code and a rare diagnosis can still point to one person.

Files carry hidden details too. A photo or scan can hold the date, the device and sometimes the place inside the file, and a file name such as "Lopez_before.jpg" names the person. Removing the visible name is not enough if the file still tells the story.

> **WARNING**  
> Never decide on your own that data is anonymous. Ask your lead or client before treating any patient-related data as safe to share.

### 2.7 Try it: the screenshot

**Scenario: A helpful screenshot**

A software tool is acting up while you work in a client's system. You want help from a friend who is good with computers. A screenshot would show the problem clearly, and it contains a patient's name and diagnosis.

- Send the full screenshot privately to your friend.  
  _That sends PHI to someone who is not authorized to see it._
- (best) Describe the problem in words, with no patient details, or report it to your lead or the client's support.  
  _Correct. Get help through approved channels and keep PHI out of it._
- Blur the name and send the rest.  
  _The diagnosis and other details can still identify the person. Do not send it._

### 2.8 Spoken and paper PHI

PHI does not stop being PHI when it is not on a screen. Calls, voice notes, printouts and conversations all count.

- Take calls where others cannot hear patient details.
- Do not leave notes with patient information in view.
- Do not print unless your client's rules allow it, and never leave printouts lying around.
- Do not discuss patients with family, friends or on social media, even without names.

### 2.9 Quick recognition

**Check yourself:** Which of these is PHI? Select all that apply. (select all that apply)

- (correct) A spreadsheet row with a patient's name and balance owed  
  _Name plus payment information: PHI._
- (correct) A voicemail from a patient asking to reschedule a dermatology visit  
  _A person's voice, their care and their request: PHI._
- A blank appointment form template with no patient data  
  _No individual is identified, so it is not PHI._
- (correct) A screenshot of a patient's insurance card  
  _An insurance number identifies the person: PHI._

## Module 3: The Privacy Rule and minimum necessary

_When patient information may be used or shared, how to keep to the minimum needed, and the ERS 5-point send check._

About 15 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials, refresher.

Key points:
- Using, disclosing and requesting information are three different actions.
- Use only the minimum information needed for the task.
- Access is by role: you see what your job needs, nothing more.
- Before you send: Who, What, Why, Where, How.
- Verify who is asking before you share anything.
- Anything beyond the permitted purposes needs the patient's written authorization, and the client decides that.
- HIPAA is the minimum: a stricter state law or client rule can apply as well.
- Reasonable safeguards cover exposure that cannot be avoided; police, courts and agencies go through the client, never through you.

### 3.1 The privacy mindset

The Privacy Rule gives patients control over their information. Your job is not to decide what is safe to share. Your job is to use the information only for the task, and to share it only through the approved way.

> **INFO**  
> A good test before any action: "Would this patient be comfortable if they saw exactly what I am doing?"

### 3.2 Use, disclosure and request

These three words mean different things, and the rules are different for each.

- **Use**: working with information inside your organization to do your task.
- **Disclosure**: giving or showing information to someone outside, for example a vendor, a family member or another office.
- **Request**: asking another organization for patient information.

Each one needs a valid reason, and each one must be limited to what is needed.

### 3.3 Treatment, payment and operations

Covered entities may use and share information for **treatment**, **payment** and **healthcare operations** without asking the patient each time. Most of the work you do falls under these.

That permission belongs to the covered entity and is passed to ERS only through the BAA. It does not make everything fair game. You work within your client's specific instructions.

> **WARNING**  
> Anything outside your task, such as marketing, a favor for a friend, research, or sharing with a family member, is a different question. Stop and ask.

### 3.4 When the patient's written permission is needed

For anything outside treatment, payment, healthcare operations and the other purposes the rule permits, the patient must give a **written authorization**: a signed form that says what may be shared, with whom, and why.

- Marketing messages that use patient information
- Sharing information with an employer or an outside company for its own purposes
- Most uses of psychotherapy notes
- Selling patient information (generally not allowed without authorization)

> **WARNING**  
> You will never decide this yourself. Your client keeps the authorizations and decides. If a request is outside your task, route it to your lead or client contact.

### 3.5 When police, a court or an agency asks

The law lets, and sometimes requires, a covered entity to share information without the patient's authorization in certain situations: when the law requires it, for public health, to report abuse or neglect, for oversight, in court proceedings, and in some law enforcement matters.

Those decisions are the client's, made with its own legal checks. A subpoena or court order, a named agency and a lawful request are things the client verifies. A phone call is not proof of any of them.

**Scenario: A caller says he is a detective**

A man phones the clinic line, says he is a detective investigating a case, and asks for a patient's home address "urgently". He gives a badge number.

- Give the address. Police have the right to it.  
  _You cannot verify him, and whether the clinic may share it is the client's decision._
- (best) Share nothing, take his contact details, and pass the request to your client contact straight away.  
  _Correct. The client verifies and decides. You route._
- Confirm only whether the person is a patient.  
  _Confirming that someone is a patient is itself a disclosure._

### 3.6 Minimum necessary

The **minimum necessary** rule means using, sharing or asking for only the least amount of information needed to do the job.

**Minimum necessary**
- Send the invoice number and amount
- Share only the dates needed to schedule
- Open the one record you were asked to update

**Too much**
- Send the full chart to answer a billing question
- Copy the entire patient list "to be safe"
- Browse other patients on the same screen

### 3.7 Where minimum necessary does not apply

There are a few exceptions, for example sharing for a patient's treatment between providers, giving the patient their own information, or disclosures the law requires.

> **TIP**  
> These exceptions are for your client to apply, not for you to decide. If you think one applies, ask your lead or client contact. Do not rely on your own reading.

### 3.8 Incidental disclosures: when it cannot be perfect

The Privacy Rule does not demand that every possible exposure be eliminated. Some exposure comes with normal work: a name on a sign-in sheet, a conversation that someone nearby might hear, a screen that a visitor glances at. These are **incidental** and are allowed, but only if **reasonable safeguards** are in place and the information was limited to the minimum.

- Keep the reason for a visit off any sheet others can read.
- Lower your voice, move away from others, or use a headset.
- Angle or lock your screen so passers-by cannot read it.
- If you could say it without a name, say it without a name.

**Check yourself:** An incidental disclosure is acceptable when:

- (correct) Reasonable safeguards were in place and only the minimum information was involved  
  _Right. Safeguards and minimum necessary are what make it incidental._
- Nobody complained  
  _Complaints are not the test. Safeguards and minimum necessary are._
- The person overhearing was a coworker  
  _Who overhears is not the test either._

### 3.9 When a patient asks for something

Patients have rights over their own information. The client handles them, and you may be the first person who hears the request.

- See or get a copy of their records
- Ask for a correction
- Get a list of certain disclosures
- Ask for limits on use or sharing, or for confidential communications
- Receive the clinic's notice of privacy practices, and complain

**Your part:** do not answer or send anything yourself. Be kind, take down only the request, and pass it on the way your client instructs. The client has its own deadlines and checks.

### 3.10 HIPAA is the minimum, not the maximum

HIPAA is a federal floor. Where a state law gives patients **more** privacy protection, that stricter law can apply as well. Some kinds of information, such as mental health, substance use treatment or HIV records, often have extra rules.

- Your client and ERS decide which rules apply to a task. Their instructions already reflect them.
- If a request involves sensitive records or something unusual, stop and ask.
- A rule being different in another state is a reason to ask, not a reason to guess.

> **TIP**  
> Whichever rule is stricter is the one you follow.

### 3.11 Role-based access

What you can see should match your job. If you find you can see more than you need, do not explore it. Tell your lead so the access can be corrected.

**Check yourself:** You notice your login can open another team's patient files. What should you do?

- (correct) Do not open them, and tell your lead  
  _Right. Extra access is a gap to be fixed, not a permission._
- Look around once to see how the system works  
  _Exploring is itself a violation._
- Ignore it and tell nobody  
  _Extra access should be reported so it can be removed._

### 3.12 Know who you are talking to

Before you share anything, you must be sure the person is allowed to receive it. A friendly voice, an official-looking email or an urgent tone is not proof.

1. **Confirm who is asking**: Use your client's verification steps, not the details the caller gives you.
2. **Confirm they are allowed**: Is this person on the approved list for this information?
3. **Share the minimum**: Only what answers the question.

### 3.13 The ERS 5-point send check

Before you send any patient information, ask five questions. If any answer is "not sure", do not send.

1. **Who?**: Is the recipient the right, authorized person?
2. **What?**: Is it only the minimum information needed?
3. **Why?**: Is there a task-related reason?
4. **Where?**: Is the destination the approved system or address?
5. **How?**: Is the method approved and secure for this information?

### 3.14 Try it: the worried daughter

**Scenario: A call from a family member**

Someone calls the clinic line and says she is a patient's daughter. She is crying and asks for her mother's appointment details and what medication was prescribed.

- Give her the details, because she is clearly upset and family.  
  _You cannot confirm she is allowed to receive them. Sympathy is not authorization._
- (best) Kindly explain you cannot share information, and follow the client's process for such requests, such as taking a message for the clinic.  
  _Correct. Stay kind, share nothing, and use the approved process._
- Share only the appointment time, not the medication.  
  _Even "just the time" is PHI. Without confirmed permission you do not share it._

## Module 4: The Security Rule: protecting electronic patient information

_What HIPAA requires to keep electronic patient information confidential, accurate and available, and your part in it._

About 13 minutes. Included in tracks: full.

Key points:
- The Security Rule protects the confidentiality, integrity and availability of electronic patient information (ePHI).
- Safeguards are administrative, physical and technical, and you are part of all three.
- Your own login and password, and multi-factor sign-in where it is used, are how access is controlled and traced.
- Activity in systems is recorded, and logging off or locking your screen is part of the rule's habits.
- The Security Rule is being updated. ERS and its clients already work the way the proposal points.

### 4.1 What security protects

The Security Rule is about keeping **electronic** patient information safe. It protects three things:

1. **Confidentiality**: Only the right people can see it.
2. **Integrity**: It is accurate and has not been changed by mistake or on purpose.
3. **Availability**: The people who need it can get to it when they need it.

> **INFO**  
> A ransomware attack that locks a clinic out of its records is a security incident even if no one reads a single record.

### 4.2 Three kinds of safeguards

The rule asks organizations to use three kinds of safeguards together:

- **Administrative**: policies, training, risk analysis, who gets access, how incidents are handled, sanctions.
- **Physical**: protecting buildings, rooms, workstations and devices.
- **Technical**: unique logins, authentication, encryption, activity logs, automatic log-off.

You are part of all three: you take the training (administrative), protect your workstation and devices (physical), and use the technical tools correctly.

### 4.3 "Required" and "addressable"

Some parts of the Security Rule are **required**. Others are **addressable**: the organization must put them in place, or document why and use an equally effective alternative. Addressable does not mean optional.

> **TIP**  
> For you the result is simple: the controls ERS and your client set up are not optional for you, whatever label the rule gives them.

### 4.4 Your login is yours alone

The rule requires every user to have a **unique user identification**, so each action in a system can be tracked to a person. If you share your login, your name is on whatever someone else does.

**Do**
- Use your own unique login
- Keep passwords private
- Use multi-factor sign-in every time it is offered
- Sign out when your work is done

**Do not**
- Share a password with a teammate
- Let anyone use your session
- Write passwords on a sticky note
- Reuse your work password elsewhere

### 4.5 Passwords and multi-factor sign-in

HIPAA's security awareness training includes **password management**: creating, changing and protecting passwords. The rule also requires systems to **verify that a person is who they say they are**.

**Multi-factor authentication (MFA)** adds a second proof, such as a code from an app. The current rule does not name MFA, but ERS and most clients require it, and HHS has proposed making it required.

> **TIP**  
> If a system asks you for a second step, that is the safeguard doing its job. Never share a code with anyone.

### 4.6 Access by role, and logging off

**Access** is limited to what each person's job needs, and it **ends when their work ends**: the rule calls for termination procedures so a former team member's accounts are closed. **Automatic log-off** ends a session after a period of inactivity so a forgotten screen does not stay open.

Do not disable, work around or extend these controls. If they get in the way of your work, tell your lead so the right fix can be made.

### 4.7 Everything is logged

Systems keep **audit logs**: who opened which record and when. The rule requires organizations to record and examine this activity. Clients and ERS review it. This is how inappropriate access, curiosity and mistakes are found.

**Check yourself:** Why does it matter that audit logs exist?

- (correct) They record who accessed what, so misuse can be found  
  _Right. Logs are how organizations notice and investigate problems._
- They let you hide your activity  
  _Logs do the opposite._
- They are only for IT staff and do not matter to you  
  _They record your actions too._

### 4.8 Sending information safely

**Transmission security** means information is protected while it travels over a network. Use only the channels your client approved: their secure portal, encrypted email, or the system they set up.

- Do not use personal email, text messages or consumer chat apps for patient information.
- Check the address twice before sending.
- Attach only what is needed, and never more than the minimum.

### 4.9 Keeping information accurate and available

**Integrity** means protecting information from being changed or destroyed improperly. Be careful when you edit or enter data: a wrong date or a wrong patient can cause real harm to care and billing.

**Availability** means people can reach the information when they need it. Backups and recovery plans exist so it can be restored after a virus, a crash or a disaster, and a data backup plan is a required part of contingency planning. Do not delete records, turn off backups or install tools unless you have been told to. If something stops working, report it.

### 4.10 What HIPAA expects you to be trained on

The rule's security awareness and training standard names four things a workforce is trained on:

1. **Security reminders**: Periodic updates on security.
2. **Protection from malicious software**: Recognizing and reporting it.
3. **Log-in monitoring**: Reporting unusual sign-in activity.
4. **Password management**: Creating, changing and protecting passwords.

> **INFO**  
> Everyday cybersecurity skills, such as spotting phishing messages, are taught in ERS's separate Cybersecurity course. If you see something that looks like an attack, report it straight away. Module 6 explains how.

### 4.11 The Security Rule is being updated

In December 2024 HHS proposed a stricter Security Rule. Among other things, it would make multi-factor authentication and encryption of electronic patient information required (with limited exceptions), remove the "addressable" category, and require written incident response plans that are tested.

It is a **proposal**. The current rule stays in effect until HHS finishes its work. ERS and its clients already use many of these practices, so your daily habits do not change.

> **TIP**  
> ERS updates this course when the rules change. Always follow the version in front of you and your client's instructions.

### 4.12 Try it: the account that should have closed

**Scenario: A former teammate is still in the system**

A teammate left ERS a month ago. While working in a client's system you notice their account is still active and was used yesterday.

- Ignore it. Someone else will have noticed.  
  _An account that should have ended and is still in use is a serious gap. Report it._
- (best) Do not use or touch it, and tell your lead and HR straight away.  
  _Correct. Access must end with the work, and a possibly misused account is a security incident._
- Use the account yourself to check what it can open.  
  _That would be using someone else's login, which is itself a violation._

- Log in, then walk away and let them use your open session.

## Module 5: Protecting patient information where you work

_How HIPAA's physical safeguards apply to your workspace: workstations, devices, approved tools, and what to do when a device goes missing._

About 9 minutes. Included in tracks: full.

Key points:
- HIPAA's physical safeguards cover how a workstation is used and the space around it.
- Use only approved devices, accounts and tools for patient information.
- Do not save, download, print, photograph or forward patient information unless your client allows it.
- Patient information must be removed properly before a device or media is reused or disposed of.
- A lost or stolen device is a security incident: report it immediately.

### 5.1 Your workstation is part of the client's workplace

The Security Rule requires policies on how workstations that can reach patient information are **used**, and on the **physical safeguards** around them. Working from home does not lower the standard: for as long as patient information is on your screen, your workspace is part of the client's workplace.

- Work in a space where others cannot see your screen or hear your calls.
- Position your screen away from windows and doorways.
- Keep family, roommates and visitors out of view of patient information.
- Lock your computer whenever you step away, even at home.
- Keep a tidy desk: no notes or printouts with patient details left out.

### 5.2 Approved systems only

Use only the systems, tools and accounts your client and ERS approved. A convenient tool is not an approved tool.

**Approved**
- The client's EHR or portal
- The email and chat your client set up
- Devices and software ERS or the client provided or approved

**Not approved**
- Personal email or messaging apps
- Free file-sharing or notes apps
- AI chatbots and online converters that receive pasted patient information

> **WARNING**  
> Pasting patient information into a public AI tool, translator or converter is a disclosure to an unauthorized party.

### 5.3 Saving, downloading, printing and photographing

The safest place for patient information is where it already is: inside the client's system. Every extra copy is another place it can leak, and another thing the client cannot control.

- Do not save patient information on your own computer unless your client allows it.
- Do not put it in personal cloud storage or notes apps.
- Do not print unless the client permits it, and return or destroy printouts as instructed.
- Do not take photos or screen recordings of patient information.
- Do not forward it to yourself "to work on later".

> **TIP**  
> This is minimum necessary in practice: use the information where it is, and do not make more copies than the task needs.

### 5.4 Devices and media

The rule requires control over the hardware and media that hold patient information: how they are received and moved, how they are **disposed of**, and how information is **removed before reuse**.

- Devices ERS or the client issued stay under their control. Do not let others use them.
- Do not connect unknown USB drives or storage devices.
- When a device is returned or retired, the client or ERS clears it the approved way. Never wipe, sell or bin work equipment yourself.
- Keep a record, if asked, of what equipment you have.

### 5.5 Lost, stolen or shared devices

If a device you use for work is lost, stolen, or used by someone else, it is a **security incident**. Tell your lead and HR immediately. Do not wait to see if it turns up.

Fast reporting lets the client lock or wipe the device and limits the harm. Delay is what turns a small problem into a serious one.

> **INFO**  
> Module 6 explains exactly what to do and what not to do when something goes wrong.

### 5.6 When you cannot work in your usual space

Sometimes your usual space or connection is not available. Your client's rules decide what is allowed: a secure connection they provide, a different private location, or waiting.

1. **Pause**: Do not improvise with patient information.
2. **Tell your lead**: A late task can be explained. A disclosure cannot be undone.
3. **Use only what is approved**: A secure option your client allows, in a private space.

> **TIP**  
> Everyday network and device habits, such as router settings, updates and public Wi-Fi, are covered in ERS's separate Cybersecurity course.

### 5.7 Try it: the cafe

**Scenario: Working away from home**

Your internet at home is down and a deadline is near. A nearby cafe has free Wi-Fi and a table by the window.

- Work there, since it is only for an hour.  
  _A public room exposes your screen, and a public network is not an approved connection._
- (best) Tell your lead, use a secure option your client allows, or wait until you are in a private space on a safe connection.  
  _Correct. Protect the information first and ask for help with the deadline._
- Work there but only on non-urgent patients.  
  _The risk is the same for any patient. The setting is the problem._

### 5.8 Try it: the shared computer

**Scenario: A shared family computer**

Your work laptop breaks. A sibling offers their computer, which the whole family uses, to finish a task.

- Use it, but sign out of everything afterward.  
  _Other people's access, software and files still make it unsafe, and it is not approved._
- (best) Do not use it. Contact your lead and the client about an approved replacement or another way.  
  _Correct. Only approved devices are used for patient information._

## Module 6: Privacy and security incidents

_What counts as an incident, why speed matters, and exactly what to do and not do when something goes wrong._

About 9 minutes. Included in tracks: full, essentials, refresher.

Key points:
- An incident is anything that may have exposed patient information or weakened its protection.
- Report immediately to your lead and HR. Do not wait to be sure.
- Do not investigate, fix quietly, delete evidence or contact the patient yourself.
- Describe what happened and when. Do not write patient details in the report.
- Good-faith reporting is protected. Nobody is punished for speaking up.

### 6.1 What is an incident?

An **incident** is anything that may have exposed, changed or blocked access to patient information, or that breaks a privacy or security rule.

- A message with patient information sent to the wrong person
- A lost or stolen laptop or phone
- A clicked phishing link or a shared password
- Records opened without a work reason
- A system that locks up or shows data that is not yours

> **INFO**  
> You do not have to be sure it is serious. If you wonder whether it is an incident, report it.

### 6.2 What usually goes wrong

Most incidents are ordinary mistakes, not attacks. These come up again and again:

- Patient information sent or faxed to the wrong person
- A laptop, phone or drive lost or stolen
- Records opened without a work reason
- A shared or borrowed login
- Paper or old devices thrown away without being destroyed
- A phishing message, virus or other attack
- Missing training or missing agreements with vendors

> **TIP: Why encryption comes up**  
> If a lost device was properly encrypted, the law may not require patients to be notified. That is the client's call, never yours. Report every loss, encrypted or not.

### 6.3 Incident and breach

A **breach** is an impermissible use or disclosure of PHI that compromises its privacy or security. The law presumes it is a breach unless a risk assessment shows a low probability that the information was compromised.

That decision belongs to the client and ERS, not to you. Your part is to **report quickly** so they can make it.

### 6.4 Why speed matters

When a breach is confirmed, the covered entity must notify the people affected without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days. A business associate must tell the covered entity first, and many contracts require it much sooner than that.

A breach counts as discovered on the first day it is known, or would have been known with reasonable care, to anyone other than the person who caused it. A few hours of delay on your side can use up time the client needs.

### 6.5 What to do

1. **Stop**: Stop what you are doing with the information. Do not try to undo it yourself.
2. **Tell**: Report to your team lead and HR immediately, and follow your client's instructions.
3. **Describe**: Say what happened, when, and which system. Leave out patient names and details.
4. **Preserve**: Keep the message, file or device as it is. Do not delete anything.
5. **Follow instructions**: Do what you are asked, and nothing more.

### 6.6 What not to do

**Do**
- Report right away
- Write facts only
- Keep evidence
- Cooperate fully

**Do not**
- Wait and see
- Fix it quietly
- Delete or edit records
- Contact the patient or discuss it with others

> **WARNING**  
> Trying to hide a mistake is far more serious than the mistake. Honest and fast is always the right answer.

### 6.7 You are protected

HIPAA prohibits retaliating against someone for filing a complaint about a possible violation or taking part in an investigation. ERS goes further: it does not punish anyone for reporting a possible incident in good faith. It does apply sanctions for deliberate violations and for hiding incidents.

**Check yourself:** You made an honest mistake and sent a message to the wrong client contact. What is the best next step?

- (correct) Report it immediately to your lead and HR  
  _Right. Fast, honest reporting is protected and limits harm._
- Wait to see if the person replies  
  _Waiting uses up time the client may need._
- Ask the person to delete it and say nothing  
  _A request is not a fix. The incident must still be reported._

### 6.8 Try it: the wrong recipient

**Scenario: It went to the wrong person**

You email a schedule with patient names to a clinic contact. A minute later you notice the address autofilled to a similarly named person at a different company.

- Send a second email asking them to ignore the first and move on.  
  _That does not report the incident, and it creates another message._
- (best) Tell your lead and HR immediately, describe what you sent and where, and follow the client's instructions.  
  _Correct. The earlier it is reported, the more can be done._
- Hope the person does not open it, and fix your autofill.  
  _Hoping is not a response, and the incident would stay hidden._

**Q37. [Incidental disclosures]** You must discuss a patient's case with a colleague and others can hear in an open office. What is the best response?

- (correct) A. Move somewhere private, or avoid using the patient's name
- B. Speak louder so the colleague hears
- C. Shout across the room to save time
- D. Post it in a group chat instead

_Reason: Reasonable safeguards include lowering your voice, moving, and leaving out names so a passer-by learns nothing._

**Q38. [Authorities]** A caller says he is from the police and demands a patient's address immediately. What do you do?

- (correct) A. Share nothing and route the request to your client contact
- B. Give it, since police have authority
- C. Confirm only that the person is a patient
- D. Ask him to email a photo of his badge to you

_Reason: The client decides whether and how to disclose, and verifies the requester. A phone call is not proof, and even confirming someone is a patient is a disclosure._

**Q39. [Mindset]** A friend who is a nurse messages you: "My neighbor went to your client's clinic. Can you check if her results are in?" What do you do?

- (correct) A. Decline, do not look, and carry on with your assigned tasks
- B. Look quickly and give a vague answer
- C. Look and tell her only that results are in
- D. Ask the neighbor's name and check later

_Reason: Looking for a personal reason is a violation and disclosing even a vague answer is a disclosure._

### The Security Rule: protecting electronic patient information (12 questions)

**Q40. [CIA]** Which three goals does the Security Rule protect? Select all that apply.

- (correct) A. Confidentiality
- (correct) B. Integrity
- (correct) C. Availability
- D. Popularity

_Reason: The Security Rule protects the confidentiality, integrity and availability of electronic PHI._

**Q41. [CIA]** A ransomware attack locks a clinic's records so staff cannot open them. Which goal is most directly affected?

- (correct) A. Availability
- B. Confidentiality
- C. Marketing
- D. Billing accuracy only

_Reason: Ransomware blocks access to information, which is a failure of availability._

**Q42. [Safeguards]** A policy that requires staff to complete HIPAA training is an example of which kind of safeguard?

- (correct) A. Administrative
- B. Physical
- C. Technical
- D. Financial

_Reason: Policies, training and procedures are administrative safeguards._

**Q43. [Safeguards]** Locking a door to the room that holds servers is an example of which kind of safeguard?

- (correct) A. Physical
- B. Administrative
- C. Technical
- D. Legal

_Reason: Protecting rooms, buildings and equipment is a physical safeguard._

**Q44. [Safeguards]** A system that makes each user sign in with their own unique login is an example of which kind of safeguard?

- (correct) A. Technical
- B. Physical
- C. Administrative
- D. Cosmetic

_Reason: Unique user identification and authentication are technical safeguards._

**Q45. [Required and addressable]** In the Security Rule, what does it mean when a specification is "addressable"?

- (correct) A. The organization must implement it, or document why and use an equally effective alternative
- B. It is optional and can be ignored
- C. Only the IT team must read it
- D. It applies only to paper records

_Reason: Addressable is not optional. The organization implements it or documents an equivalent alternative._

**Q46. [Credentials]** A teammate's account is locked and a deadline is near. They ask to use your login for an hour. What do you do?

- (correct) A. Decline, and help them reach support or your lead to fix their account
- B. Share it and change the password later
- C. Share it only if they promise not to open anything else
- D. Stay logged in and step away while they use it

_Reason: Logins are personal. Sharing puts your name on their actions and breaks access controls and audit logs._

**Q47. [Passwords]** Which is part of HIPAA's security awareness training for the workforce?

- (correct) A. Password management: creating, changing and protecting passwords
- B. Choosing which patients to call
- C. Setting prices for services
- D. Writing marketing messages

_Reason: The security awareness and training standard includes password management, security reminders, malicious software and log-in monitoring._

**Q48. [Audit logs]** True or false: Access to patient records is logged, so looking at a record without a work reason can be found later.

- (correct) A. True
- B. False

_Reason: Audit logs record who opened what and when, and are reviewed to detect misuse._

**Q49. [Transmission]** Which is an appropriate way to send patient information?

- (correct) A. Through the secure channel your client approved
- B. By a personal text message
- C. Through a personal email account
- D. In a public group chat

_Reason: Use only approved, secure channels. Personal email, texts and consumer chat apps are not approved._

**Q50. [Access ends with work]** You see that a former teammate's account in a client system is still active. What should you do?

- (correct) A. Leave it alone and report it to your lead and HR right away
- B. Use it, since it is still working
- C. Ignore it
- D. Delete the account yourself

_Reason: Access must end with the work (termination procedures). An account that should be closed but is still active needs to be reported, not used or fixed quietly._

**Q51. [The rule is changing]** True or false: The stricter Security Rule that HHS proposed is already in effect, replacing the current rule.

- A. True
- (correct) B. False

_Reason: It is a proposal. The current Security Rule remains in effect while HHS works on it._

### Protecting patient information where you work (10 questions)

**Q52. [Workstation]** Which setup best protects patient information at home?

- (correct) A. A private space where others cannot see your screen or hear your calls
- B. A shared living room with the television on
- C. A busy cafe
- D. A sofa near the front door

_Reason: The workstation rule is about how a workstation is used and the surroundings. A private space prevents others from seeing or hearing patient information._

**Q53. [Approved systems]** You want to translate a paragraph that contains patient details. Which is the right choice?

- (correct) A. Do not paste it into an online tool; ask your lead for an approved way
- B. Paste it into a free online translator
- C. Paste it into a public AI chatbot
- D. Email it to yourself and translate it later

_Reason: Pasting patient information into a public tool discloses it to an unauthorized party._

**Q54. [Approved systems]** Which of these are NOT approved ways to handle patient information? Select all that apply.

- (correct) A. Personal email
- (correct) B. A free notes app on your phone
- C. The client's approved portal
- (correct) D. A personal cloud storage folder

_Reason: Personal email, consumer notes apps and personal cloud storage are not approved. The client's portal is._

**Q55. [Screen lock]** You need to get a glass of water at home for two minutes while a patient record is open. What should you do?

- (correct) A. Lock your screen first
- B. Leave it as is since you are home
- C. Turn the screen brightness down
- D. Minimize the window

_Reason: A locked screen prevents others in the home from seeing or using the session._

**Q56. [Working away]** Your home internet is down and you have urgent work with patient information. What is the best choice?

- (correct) A. Tell your lead and wait for a secure connection or use an option your client allows
- B. Use free public Wi-Fi at a cafe
- C. Use a neighbor's open network
- D. Share patient information through a personal phone

_Reason: Public and open networks and rooms are not approved for patient information. Ask for help and use only allowed secure options._

**Q57. [Storing]** True or false: It is fine to save patient information to your own computer's desktop if you delete it at the end of the day.

- A. True
- (correct) B. False

_Reason: Do not save patient information locally unless your client allows it. Keep it in the approved system._

**Q58. [Photos]** A bug appears on your screen with a patient chart open. A teammate asks you to take a picture with your phone. What do you do?

- (correct) A. Decline and report the problem through the approved channel without patient details
- B. Take the photo and send it privately
- C. Take the photo and blur the name
- D. Record a video of the whole screen

_Reason: Photos of patient information on a personal device are unapproved copies of PHI._

**Q59. [Lost device]** You notice your work laptop is missing after a trip. What is the right action?

- (correct) A. Tell your lead and HR immediately
- B. Wait a few days in case it turns up
- C. Buy a new one and say nothing
- D. Post on social media asking for help

_Reason: A lost device is a security incident. Immediate reporting lets the client lock or wipe it and limits harm._

**Q60. [Shared devices]** Your work laptop breaks and a family member offers their shared computer. What should you do?

- (correct) A. Do not use it and ask your lead for an approved option
- B. Use it and log out afterward
- C. Use it only for non-urgent tasks
- D. Use it in a private browser window

_Reason: Only approved devices should be used for patient information. A shared home computer is not approved._

**Q61. [Disposal and reuse]** A laptop issued for client work is being returned and will be reused by someone else. What should happen to the patient information on it?

- (correct) A. The client or ERS removes it in the approved way before reuse
- B. Nothing, since the next user is also an ERS employee
- C. You delete the files you remember and send it back
- D. You keep a copy first in case it is needed

_Reason: The Security Rule requires information to be removed from media before reuse and for disposal to be handled properly. It is done in the approved way, not by individuals improvising._

### Privacy and security incidents (12 questions)

**Q62. [What is an incident]** Which of these should be reported as a possible privacy or security incident? Select all that apply.

- (correct) A. An email with patient information sent to the wrong recipient
- (correct) B. A lost laptop used for work
- (correct) C. A password you accidentally shared
- D. A reminder to take a break

_Reason: Misdirected PHI, lost devices and shared passwords are incidents. A break reminder is not._

**Q63. [Speed]** You just realized you sent a file with patient names to the wrong person. When should you report it?

- (correct) A. Immediately
- B. At the end of the week
- C. Only if the person replies
- D. After you have checked with a friend

_Reason: Speed matters. Report immediately so the client can act within its deadlines._

**Q64. [Who to tell]** Who should you tell first about a possible incident?

- (correct) A. Your team lead and HR, following your client's instructions
- B. The patient directly
- C. Your coworkers in a group chat
- D. Nobody until you are sure

_Reason: Report through the proper channel. Do not tell the patient or wait for certainty._

**Q65. [Do not]** A teammate tells you they deleted an email that revealed a mistake to avoid trouble. What is the right response?

- (correct) A. Encourage them to report it and keep any evidence
- B. Say nothing
- C. Help them delete related files
- D. Tell them to deny everything

_Reason: Deleting evidence and hiding incidents makes things worse. Report and preserve._

**Q66. [Breach]** Who decides whether an incident is a reportable breach?

- (correct) A. The client and ERS, through their risk assessment
- B. The person who made the mistake
- C. A coworker
- D. The patient

_Reason: A breach determination follows a formal risk assessment by the responsible organization. You report; they decide._

**Q67. [Breach]** Under HIPAA, a covered entity must notify affected individuals of a breach without unreasonable delay and no later than:

- (correct) A. 60 days after discovery
- B. 6 months
- C. 1 year
- D. Whenever convenient

_Reason: The outer limit for individual notification is 60 days from discovery, and delay should not be unreasonable._

**Q68. [Report details]** What should you include when you write an incident report?

- (correct) A. What happened, when and where, with no patient names or details
- B. The full patient record
- C. Your guess about whose fault it was
- D. Only that something went wrong

_Reason: Give facts about the event. Keep patient details out of the report itself._

**Q69. [No retaliation]** True or false: HIPAA prohibits an organization from retaliating against someone for filing a complaint about a possible violation.

- (correct) A. True
- B. False

_Reason: The Privacy Rule bars intimidating or retaliatory acts against people who file complaints or take part in investigations. ERS also does not punish good-faith internal reports._

**Q70. [Preserve]** You suspect a phishing email caused a problem. What should you do with the email?

- (correct) A. Keep it and report it
- B. Delete it immediately
- C. Forward it to everyone
- D. Print it and throw it away

_Reason: Do not delete evidence. The message helps responders understand what happened._

**Q71. [Common causes]** Which of these are common causes of privacy or security incidents? Select all that apply.

- (correct) A. Information sent to the wrong person
- (correct) B. A lost or stolen laptop
- (correct) C. Records opened without a work reason
- D. Taking a regular break

_Reason: Misdirected information, lost devices and unauthorized access are among the most common causes. A break is not an incident._

**Q72. [Encryption]** A work laptop is stolen from a car. It has full-disk encryption. What do you do?

- (correct) A. Report it immediately; the client decides whether notification is needed
- B. Say nothing, because it was encrypted
- C. Decide yourself that no report is needed
- D. Wait a week to see if it is found

_Reason: Encryption can mean notification is not required, but that decision belongs to the client and ERS. Every loss is reported._

**Q73. [Scenario]** You find a printout with patient names left on a shared printer at a co-working space. What do you do?

- (correct) A. Keep it secure, report it to your lead and HR, and follow instructions
- B. Throw it in the bin
- C. Leave it where it is
- D. Take a photo and post it to ask whose it is

_Reason: Secure the document, then report it. Throwing it away or posting a photo spreads the exposure._

### Scenario lab and readiness check (10 questions)

**Q74. [Scenario]** A coworker from a different client team asks you to send them your clinic's appointment list "just for formatting ideas". What do you do?

- (correct) A. Decline and suggest a sample with no patient information
- B. Send it, because they also work at ERS
- C. Send it with last names removed
- D. Send it by personal email so it is not logged

_Reason: Being at the same company is not a need to know. First names and appointments can still identify patients._

**Q75. [Scenario]** Before sending a billing summary, you notice the email program autofilled a similar address you do not recognize. What do you do?

- (correct) A. Check the address against the client's contact list and correct it first
- B. Send it and fix it if anyone complains
- C. Send it to both addresses
- D. Send it without the attachment and add it later

_Reason: Misdirected email is a common cause of incidents. Verify the destination before sending._

**Q76. [Scenario]** A friend comes into your workspace while a patient list is on your screen. What is the best response?

- (correct) A. Lock the screen or move before they see it
- B. Keep working and angle the monitor slightly
- C. Ask them to look away
- D. Cover the screen with a book

_Reason: The safe action is to prevent exposure by locking the screen or moving to a private space._

**Q77. [Scenario]** A caller says he is from a vendor and needs your password to fix a problem. What should you do?

- (correct) A. Hang up and report the call to your lead
- B. Give the password but ask them to call back
- C. Give a different password
- D. Ask for their phone number and call them later

_Reason: Real support never needs your password. This is a social engineering attempt and should be reported._

**Q78. [Scenario]** Late at night you realize you saved a patient file to your personal cloud storage earlier. What should you do?

- (correct) A. Report it right away to your lead and HR and follow their instructions
- B. Delete it and say nothing
- C. Wait until morning in case it was nothing
- D. Move it to another personal folder

_Reason: A report is needed even when you delete the copy, because the exposure may already have happened._

**Q79. [Scenario]** A patient emails you asking for their test results because they forgot their portal password. What do you do?

- (correct) A. Share nothing and point them to the client's approved process
- B. Reply with the results
- C. Ask for a copy of their ID by email
- D. Call the number in the email signature and read the results

_Reason: You cannot verify the sender and email is not an approved channel. Use the client's process._

**Q80. [Scenario]** Your client's instructions say to send claim files only through the secure portal, but a coworker suggests email "because it is faster". What do you do?

- (correct) A. Use the portal as instructed
- B. Use email this once
- C. Use a personal messaging app
- D. Split the file into smaller parts and email them

_Reason: Client instructions define approved channels. Convenience does not override them._

**Q81. [Scenario]** You spot your own name on a patient list because you are also a patient at the client's clinic. What may you do with your own record?

- (correct) A. Nothing beyond your assigned task; use the clinic's patient process for your own information
- B. Open your record and check it
- C. Share your record with your family
- D. Update it yourself

_Reason: Your work access is not for personal use, even for your own record. Use the clinic's patient process._

**Q82. [Scenario]** A message in a client chat group asks everyone to "reply with your password to confirm your account". What do you do?

- (correct) A. Do not reply, and report it
- B. Reply because it is in the official group
- C. Reply with an old password
- D. Reply with only the first half of your password

_Reason: No legitimate service requests passwords this way, and group chats can be compromised. Report it._

**Q83. [Scenario]** Your lead asks you to prepare a spreadsheet with only the invoice number and amount. You also have the patients' names available. What should the spreadsheet contain?

- (correct) A. Only the invoice number and amount
- B. Invoice number, amount and patient names for convenience
- C. Everything, filtered later by the recipient
- D. A copy of the full billing report

_Reason: Minimum necessary: provide only what the task needs._
